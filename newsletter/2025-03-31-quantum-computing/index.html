March brought conflicting narratives in quantum computing: D-Wave claimed the first practical quantum supremacy demonstration, China unveiled a processor reportedly quadrillions of times faster than supercomputers, and Microsoft faced fierce criticism over its topological qubit breakthrough. Meanwhile, the industry poured billions into scaling hardware, with PsiQuantum raising $750 million and NVIDIA opening a dedicated quantum research center.

This comprehensive review covers 45 articles published in March 2025, surveying the latest developments in quantum computing.

The Skeptical Breakthrough¶

In February, Microsoft unveiled Majorana 1, a quantum chip with a Topological Core architecture that theoretically enables stable and scalable qubits using topoconductors—materials that host exotic Majorana particles first theorized in 1937. The chip contains 8 topological qubits, and Microsoft says this architecture paves the way for million-qubit machines capable of solving complex problems within years.

But physicists aren’t buying it. A Nature report published March 18 documents widespread skepticism, with researchers saying the evidence is insufficient to support the claims. Henry Legg, a physicist at the University of St. Andrews, challenged the validity of a key test underlying Microsoft’s claims. Sergey Frolov, a professor at the University of Pittsburgh, called the project “unreliable” and cited a lack of established physics in Microsoft’s work.

Critics note this isn’t the first time Microsoft’s Majorana research has faced scrutiny—the company retracted a 2018 paper claiming detection of Majorana particles. The controversy echoes the field’s broader reproducibility crisis: extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, and Microsoft’s latest work has not convinced many experts.

At the American Physical Society’s Global Physics Summit on March 19, Chetan Nayak, a Microsoft researcher, presented new data defending the work. But hundreds of physicists attending the talk remained unconvinced, with some arguing the data looks like noise and the methods are flawed. Microsoft stands by its research and continues development, but the episode underscores the high standards required for breakthrough claims in quantum computing.

Three Supremacy Claims, Different Approaches¶

While Microsoft’s claims faced skepticism, three other groups announced quantum performance claims—each using different technologies, different benchmarks, and different levels of peer review.

D-Wave: First Practical Supremacy?¶

D-Wave published peer-reviewed results in Science showing its Advantage2 annealing quantum computer solved a materials simulation problem (a spin glass system) in minutes that would take the Frontier supercomputer approximately 1 million years to complete. This wasn’t a random benchmark—it was a practical scientific problem that researchers actually care about.

The study, led by researchers including Andrew King from D-Wave and Daniel Lidar from the University of Southern California, represents the first peer-reviewed demonstration of what the authors call “practical quantum supremacy”—solving a problem of practical significance faster than any classical computer could. The work built on D-Wave’s previous research using a reverse annealing protocol that allows the system to refine solutions iteratively.

Jared Rovny, a physicist at D-Wave, noted that the performance gap is so large that classical computers would need to improve by many orders of magnitude to compete. Annealing quantum computers like D-Wave’s are fundamentally different from gate-based systems—they excel at optimization problems rather than general-purpose computation, which explains why this result came on a practical materials science problem rather than an abstract benchmark.

China’s Zuchongzhi-3: Speedup on Random Circuits¶

Chinese researchers from the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC) unveiled Zuchongzhi 3.0, a 105-qubit superconducting processor that reportedly performs random circuit sampling (RCS)—a standard quantum benchmark—2 million billion times faster than the Frontier supercomputer. The results were published as a cover article in Physical Review Letters.

The processor achieved:

  • 99.90% parallel single-qubit gate fidelity
  • 99.62% parallel two-qubit gate fidelity
  • 72 microsecond coherence times
  • 182 couplers connecting the qubits in a 2D grid architecture

On the RCS benchmark with 83 qubits and 32 circuit layers, Zuchongzhi 3.0 completed the task in a few hundred seconds. The researchers estimate it would take Frontier approximately 6.4 billion years to simulate the same computation—a speedup factor of roughly \(2 \times 10^{15}\), or 15 orders of magnitude.

However, experts note this benchmark favors quantum methods by design. RCS doesn’t solve a practical problem—it’s designed to be hard for classical computers. Additionally, classical algorithms have improved dramatically since Google’s 2019 supremacy claim, with GPU-based simulations completing Google’s original task in 14 seconds. The Zuchongzhi results push the frontier further, but the practical applications remain limited.

Pan Jianwei, Zhu Xiaobo, and Chengzhi Peng led the USTC team, which has previously demonstrated quantum supremacy with both the Jiuzhang photonic systems and earlier Zuchongzhi superconducting processors.

Certified Randomness: A Provably Quantum Task¶

A collaboration between Quantinuum, the University of Texas at Austin, JPMorganChase, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, and Argonne National Laboratory achieved something truly new: certified randomness that is provably impossible for classical computers to generate.

Using a 56-qubit Quantinuum H2 trapped-ion quantum computer, the team applied a protocol first proposed by Scott Aaronson in 2018 to generate random numbers that were then mathematically verified by classical supercomputers with a combined 1.1 exaflops of sustained performance. The team certified 71,313 bits of entropy—randomness that can be mathematically proven to be genuinely unpredictable.

The research, published in Nature on March 26, represents a practical quantum application with real-world utility. Certified randomness is crucial for:

  • Cryptography: Generating unguessable encryption keys
  • Fairness: Ensuring truly random selections in lotteries and elections
  • Privacy: Creating random numbers that cannot be predicted or manipulated

Unlike the other supremacy claims, certified randomness isn’t about speed—it’s about capability. Classical computers provably cannot generate this type of mathematically verifiable randomness, making it a fundamental demonstration of quantum advantage.

Rajeeb Hazra, CEO of Quantinuum, called the milestone “a practical application of quantum computing that is beyond the reach of classical systems.”

Hardware Advances: Scaling and Error Correction¶

Beyond the headline-grabbing supremacy claims, March brought significant advances in quantum hardware—particularly in error correction, the bottleneck that determines whether large-scale quantum computing becomes practical.

Error Correction Breakthroughs¶

Fujitsu, QuTech, and TU Delft set a new world record by achieving quantum gate error rates below 0.1% using a two-qubit system built on diamond spin qubits. Some gates achieved error rates as low as 0.001%. The researchers used ultrapure synthetic diamonds from Element Six and designed quantum gates to decouple qubits from environmental noise.

This milestone is significant because error correction requires physical qubits with error rates low enough that logical qubits can be constructed with reasonable overhead. The diamond spin qubits operate at 10 Kelvin, warmer than the millikelvin temperatures required by superconducting qubits, and the team plans to scale up by adding more nuclear spins and developing optical interconnects for networking.

In late February, Amazon Web Services (AWS) unveiled Ocelot, its first prototype quantum computing chip focused on error correction. Ocelot uses novel “bosonic cat qubits”—a type of qubit encoded in harmonic oscillator states that are naturally resistant to bit-flip errors. The chip uses a one-dimensional repetition code that AWS claims reduces error correction overhead by up to 90% compared to traditional surface codes.

The numbers are striking:

  • Traditional approach: ~49 physical qubits needed for one logical qubit
  • Ocelot approach: 9 physical qubits needed (5 data qubits, 4 ancilla qubits)

Ocelot’s cat qubits maintain bit-flip stability for 1 second—orders of magnitude longer than the 50-100 microsecond coherence times of traditional transmon qubits. The phase-flip time is 20 microseconds, which the system corrects through its repetition code architecture. This hardware-efficient approach could dramatically accelerate progress toward fault-tolerant quantum systems.

Architecture Innovations¶

MIT researchers developed a novel quantum interconnect that enables all-to-all communication between superconducting quantum processors. The team demonstrated remote entanglement between two processors using a superconducting waveguide, achieving over 60% photon absorption efficiency. This interconnect paves the way for scalable quantum networks and distributed quantum computing by allowing modules to communicate directly without complex routing architectures.

NTT Corporation and collaborators proposed a new load-store architecture for fault-tolerant quantum computing that separates memory and processor, similar to classical computer architecture. This approach achieves:

  • Up to 100% asymptotic memory efficiency
  • ~90% practical memory efficiency
  • Only a ~3% increase in computation time compared to ideal systems

This represents a significant improvement over conventional quantum computers, which achieve only 44-67% memory efficiency. The architecture enables more efficient use of quantum hardware and improves program portability across different quantum systems.

Researchers are also exploring alternatives to traditional qubits. Nature highlighted the emerging field of “qudits”—quantum information units that can exist in three or more simultaneous states (compared to qubits’ two states). Qudits offer increased information density and could reduce the number of quantum elements needed for certain computations.

Room-Temperature and Modular Systems¶

Xanadu launched Aurora, the world’s first modular, room-temperature photonic quantum computer. The system uses:

  • 35 interconnected photonic chips
  • 8 miles (13 kilometers) of fiber optic cables
  • Room-temperature operation—no cryogenic cooling required

Photonic quantum computing uses particles of light rather than matter-based qubits, enabling room-temperature operation and fiber-optic networking. Xanadu’s modular approach tackles scalability and error correction by allowing the system to be expanded through additional modules. The Nature-published architecture could enable practical quantum data centers and new applications in molecular simulation and cryptography.

Ecosystem Development: OS, Networking, and AI Integration¶

As hardware advances, the quantum ecosystem is maturing with new operating systems, networking capabilities, and integration with classical AI computing.

Quantum Operating Systems¶

OQTOPUS (Open Quantum Toolchain for Operators and Users), an open-source quantum computer operating system, launched on GitHub from a consortium led by Osaka University, Fujitsu, SEC, and TIS. The OS covers the full stack from quantum computer setup to operation, lowering the barrier to deploying quantum computers via the cloud. Fujitsu plans to make OQTOPUS available to research partners in the second half of 2025.

Separately, researchers published QNodeOS in Nature—a quantum network operating system that enables platform-independent execution of quantum network applications in high-level software. The system was demonstrated on quantum network nodes based on:

  • Nitrogen-vacancy centers in diamond (2 nodes)
  • Trapped-ion systems (1 node with a single 40Ca+ atom)

QNodeOS allows multitasking and efficient hardware use, abstracting underlying physical complexities so users can execute applications without detailed knowledge of the specific quantum systems. This work, described as a “huge practical step forward” in a Nature commentary , provides a critical abstraction layer for the emerging quantum internet.

AI + Quantum¶

NVIDIA announced the NVIDIA Accelerated Quantum Research Center (NVAQC) in Boston to integrate AI supercomputing with quantum computing. The center will deploy 576 Blackwell GPUs and use Quantum-2 InfiniBand networking to tackle challenges like qubit noise and error correction. Partners include Quantinuum, Quantum Machines, QuEra Computing, Harvard University, and MIT.

Interestingly, NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang recently estimated that practical quantum computers are likely 20 years away, noting a gap of 5-6 orders of magnitude in qubit count needed for useful systems. Yet NVIDIA is investing now to develop the hybrid quantum-classical systems that will bridge that gap.

On the theoretical side, Google Quantum AI researchers published work showing quantum algorithms could learn “periodic neuron” neural networks exponentially faster than classical methods. The proposed approach uses a modified quantum Fourier transform within a Quantum Statistical Query learning model to find hidden patterns in data. This work is purely theoretical—it doesn’t demonstrate a practical speedup on current hardware—but identifies a specific problem class where quantum computers might offer provable advantages.

Industry-Academia Partnerships¶

In Germany, Fraunhofer IAO, Fraunhofer IPA, and the University of Stuttgart established “flaQship”, a cross-institute lab to pool research expertise in applied quantum computing. The lab focuses on quantum machine learning, material simulation, and error correction, aiming to create value for companies and society. The collaboration will organize the ML4QT symposium in July 2025 to advance quantum technology through machine learning.

Money and Momentum: The Investment Surge¶

March revealed the massive capital flowing into quantum computing, with venture funding setting records and governments making strategic bets.

Mega-Rounds¶

PsiQuantum is raising at least $750 million at a $6 billion valuation to fund its development of million-qubit, fault-tolerant photonic quantum computers. The company is leveraging traditional chip manufacturing at GlobalFoundries and has secured major public partnerships:

  • $500 million from the State of Illinois, Cook County, and Chicago for a U.S. quantum computer project
  • $617 million (AUD $940 million) from Australian governments for a Brisbane computer

PsiQuantum targets 2029 to deliver its first commercial system, positioning it as a competitor to tech giants pursuing different qubit technologies.

China launched a massive $138 billion government-backed venture fund to invest in emerging technologies including quantum computing, AI, and semiconductors. The fund aims to commercialize research and enhance technological self-reliance, dwarfing Western initiatives:

  • U.S. National Quantum Initiative: $1.2 billion over five years (2018), with $2.7 billion proposed for reauthorization
  • Europe’s Quantum Flagship: ~$1.1 billion over 10 years

IonQ raised $372.6 million through a share sale, bringing its total cash balance to over $700 million. The funding follows strategic moves including acquiring a majority stake in quantum networking provider ID Quantique and forming a partnership with SK Telecom. IonQ also commissioned a quantum networking system at the U.S. Air Force Research Laboratory as part of over $94.4 million in contracts.

Growth Funding¶

Smaller but strategically important rounds included:

  • QuamCore (Israel): $9 million seed funding for architecture enabling 1 million superconducting qubits in a single cryostat by reducing cabling by a factor of 1,000
  • QuantWare (Netherlands): 20 million Series A for VIO technology to scale quantum processors to 1 million+ qubits; also launched pre-orders for “Contralto-A” QPU, nearly twice as large as competing solutions
  • Singapore: $18.3 million SGD for HQCC 1.0, a national initiative to integrate quantum and high-performance computing
  • Australia: $15 million government investment in QuintessenceLabs for quantum cybersecurity
  • Iceberg Quantum: $2 million pre-seed and partnership with PsiQuantum to apply quantum LDPC codes for error correction overhead reduction

The broader trend is clear: 2024 set a new venture funding record for quantum computing startups at $1.9 billion, up 138% from 2023’s $789 million, according to Crunchbase data . Momentum continues into 2025 with massive individual rounds including QuEra Computing’s $230 million and Quantum Machines’ $170 million.

Not everyone is keeping pace. A report in The Times warns the UK risks falling behind due to government spending delays, despite strong research from institutions like Oxford University and startups like Oxford Ionics (which has raised 37 million and employs 80 engineers). Competitors including the U.S. and Germany are moving faster with larger contracts.

What March Really Means¶

Stepping back from individual news items, three patterns emerge from March’s developments:

First, quantum supremacy claims are diversifying. D-Wave demonstrated advantage on a practical materials simulation problem using annealing. China’s Zuchongzhi-3 showed massive speedup on random circuit sampling. Quantinuum and partners proved certified randomness is classically impossible. These aren’t competing claims on a single benchmark—they’re demonstrations of different quantum approaches excelling at different tasks. The field isn’t a single race anymore; it’s branching into multiple technological paths.

Second, skepticism is healthy. The controversy around Microsoft’s Majorana 1 chip shows the field is maturing and demanding rigorous evidence. After past retractions and exaggerated claims, physicists are rightfully insisting on extraordinary evidence for extraordinary breakthroughs. This skepticism doesn’t slow progress—it strengthens it by ensuring resources flow to reproducible, verifiable advances.

Third, the real story is money. $1.9 billion in 2024 VC funding (up 138%), massive government investments like China’s $138 billion fund, and huge bets on specific approaches like PsiQuantum’s $750 million for photonics and D-Wave’s annealing systems. The debate has shifted from “will quantum work?” to “which approach will scale?”

March didn’t resolve the fundamental questions in quantum computing. Error correction remains challenging, practical applications are still emerging, and timelines for fault-tolerant systems span years to decades. But the month clarified the stakes: companies and countries are placing substantial bets on different technological approaches, hardware is making measurable progress on error correction and scaling, and the ecosystem is developing the software and infrastructure needed to make quantum computers usable.

The race isn’t about a single breakthrough anymore—it’s about which combination of qubit technology, error correction approach, and ecosystem development reaches practical scale first. March brought progress on all three fronts.
