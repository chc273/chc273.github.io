Meta’s acquisition of Manus for $2 billion signals a new phase in the AI agent race. As major tech companies rush to standardize how agents work, enterprises grapple with security risks while researchers demonstrate capabilities that rival human experts.

This comprehensive review covers 267 articles published between December 2025 and January 2026, surveying the latest developments in AI agents.

The Big Story: Meta’s $2 Billion Bet¶

Meta acquired Manus, a Singapore-based AI agent startup, for approximately $2 billion in the largest agentic AI acquisition to date. The deal marks a significant validation of the agent market and accelerates competitive pressure across the industry.

Manus, a China-founded company that relocated to Singapore, had reached $100 million in annual recurring revenue just eight months after launching its general-purpose AI agent according to industry reports. The company built autonomous agents capable of executing complex tasks across enterprise workflows, positioning itself as a platform for “digital coworkers” rather than single-purpose tools.

The acquisition carries geopolitical complexity. Meta committed to severing all of Manus’s remaining ties with China post-acquisition, addressing potential regulatory concerns. CEO Xiao Hong founded Manus in China before moving operations to Singapore, a shift that became a selling point amid U.S. scrutiny of Chinese AI technology.

Manus’s technology enables agents to work autonomously for extended periods, planning and executing multi-step workflows with minimal human intervention. Meta plans to integrate Manus’s capabilities across its family of apps, potentially bringing agent functionality to WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook’s billions of users.

The announcement moved Meta’s stock higher, as investors interpreted the deal as acceleration rather than desperation. At just eight months old when acquired, Manus’s $100 million ARR represents one of the fastest revenue ramps in AI startup history, though analysis noted the premium valuation reflects FOMO as much as fundamentals.

More broadly, the Manus acquisition signals the start of consolidation in agentic AI. After two years of frenetic startup activity, platforms are buying capabilities rather than building them. For Meta specifically, it addresses a gap in its AI portfolio—while the company invested heavily in models (Llama) and consumer apps, it lacked a strong agent platform to compete with OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google.

Industry Moves to Standardize the Agent Era¶

In a remarkable display of cooperation among fierce competitors, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Block formed the Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation. The initiative aims to establish open standards for how AI agents interact with each other and with external systems.

OpenAI donated its AGENTS.md specification, while Anthropic contributed the Model Context Protocol (MCP), an open standard for connecting AI applications to external data and tools. Both become vendor-neutral projects under the foundation’s governance, with support from Google, Microsoft, AWS, Bloomberg, and Cloudflare.

The standardization push addresses a growing bottleneck: agent interoperability. As enterprises deploy agents from multiple vendors, the lack of common protocols creates integration complexity. MCP currently powers 75 connectors for Claude alone, and that number will grow as the protocol becomes a community standard rather than a proprietary technology.

Notably, Anthropic simultaneously launched Agent Skills, a competing standard framework for enterprise agent capabilities. The dual moves—donating MCP while introducing Agent Skills—highlight the tension between collaboration and competition. The Linux Foundation effort focuses on plumbing (how agents connect), while Agent Skills addresses capabilities (what agents can do).

For developers, the foundation promises more portable agent code. Today, agents built for one platform often require significant rework to run on another. Open standards could enable “write once, run anywhere” agent development similar to how container standards transformed cloud computing. The foundation’s first deliverables will include standardized agent-to-agent communication protocols and tool interfaces.

The irony of OpenAI and Anthropic collaborating on infrastructure while competing fiercely on products wasn’t lost on observers. As Wired noted, “The companies trying to build the most powerful AI agents are also the ones trying to prevent those agents from causing chaos.” Standardization, they argue, is a prerequisite for safety at scale.

Platform Wars: Google, Amazon, Microsoft Unveil Agent Offerings¶

Google: From Search to Personal Agent¶

Google announced a reimagined Deep Research agent based on Gemini 3 Pro, launching the same day OpenAI released GPT-5.2. The timing was coincidental but symbolic of the intensifying race. Google’s agent enables developers to embed research capabilities directly into applications via the Interactions API, making multi-step information gathering a building block rather than a standalone product.

Separately, Google Labs unveiled CC, a personal assistant agent that integrates with Gmail, Calendar, and Drive to provide daily briefings and task assistance. The agent delivers morning summaries of emails, schedules, and documents—essentially automating the “scroll through apps” routine that starts many workdays. CC launches in early access for AI Ultra subscribers in the U.S. and Canada.

On the efficiency front, Google researchers developed a framework that makes AI agents more efficient by making them aware of their own uncertainty. Agents that know when they’re stuck avoid wasting compute on unlikely solutions, reducing costs and latency. The Interactions API extends this capability to developers, enabling faster agent responses.

For developers, Google released the Agent Development Kit for TypeScript, providing code-first tools for building production agents. The company also launched A2UI, an open project for agent-driven interfaces that lets agents build user interfaces on the fly. Additionally, Google introduced managed MCP servers that let AI agents plug directly into Google’s tools and services.

Amazon: Agents for Code and Business Processes¶

Amazon Web Services previewed three new “frontier agents” headlined by Kiro, an autonomous coding agent that can work for days without human intervention. Unlike coding assistants that require continuous prompting, Kiro maintains context across sessions and learns from human interactions, taking on larger tasks like implementing features from specifications.

AWS also announced Amazon Nova Act, now generally available , a service for building agents that automate browser-based workflows. Nova Act promises over 90% task reliability at scale by using reinforcement learning in synthetic environments—essentially training agents in simulated web gyms before exposing them to real websites. The service handles form filling, data extraction, shopping and booking, and QA testing.

For orchestration, AWS introduced Strands Agents, a framework for building multi-agent workflows without custom integration work. Strands connects naturally with Nova Act, enabling comprehensive automation from data gathering to UI execution.

In financial services, AWS published guidance on choosing the right multi-agent patterns, reflecting the company’s focus on enterprise use cases. The company also detailed how to connect API Gateway to AgentCore Gateway with MCP, streamlining tool interactions.

Microsoft: Copilot Evolves into an Agent Platform¶

At Microsoft Ignite 2025, the company showcased enhancements to its Copilot agent platform, including management and governance capabilities through Agent 365 . Microsoft introduced Agent Lightning, a system for adding reinforcement learning to agents without code rewrites, enabling agents to improve from experience.

In research collaborations, Microsoft partnered with Oxford on a multi-agent cancer care assistant and joined NASA on agents for flood prediction. The company also launched the Agentic Launchpad, an accelerator program for AI agent startups.

However, Microsoft faces enterprise selling challenges . Azure salespeople are reportedly struggling to meet ambitious targets as AI agents frequently fail real-world tasks. Google’s Gemini 2.5 Pro fails approximately 70% of real-world office tasks according to one report, and similar issues plague competitors. The gap between demo capabilities and production reliability remains a significant barrier to enterprise adoption.

On the privacy front, Microsoft confirmed that Windows 11 will ask for consent before AI agents can access personal files, following user outrage over initial plans for broader access. The company also added agent launchers to Windows 11 via the KB5072043 update.

Research: AI Agents Match Human Performance¶

Cybersecurity: ARTEMIS Outperforms Humans¶

Stanford University’s ARTEMIS AI agent placed second out of ten in a penetration testing competition, outperforming nine of ten human participants. Running at $18 per hour compared to $60 per hour for a professional penetration tester, ARTEMIS discovered nine vulnerabilities in the university’s 8,000-device network during the 10-hour evaluation period.

The key to ARTEMIS’s success was its ability to generate “sub-agents” that investigated vulnerabilities in parallel while the main agent continued scanning. Humans, by contrast, had to investigate each vulnerability sequentially before moving on. However, the study noted ARTEMIS still missed some vulnerabilities identified by humans and occasionally required hints.

Existing AI cybersecurity agents from OpenAI and Anthropic underperformed in the study, with OpenAI’s Codex and Anthropic’s Claude Code lacking cybersecurity-specific design. The models either refused to look for vulnerabilities or stalled, highlighting the gap between general-purpose and specialized agents.

Scientific Research: Agents Run Real Experiments¶

IIT Delhi developed AILA (Artificially Intelligent Lab Assistant), an agent that autonomously designs, conducts, and analyzes laboratory experiments. Using an Agentic AI Framework to replicate human scientist reasoning, AILA reduces experimental time from hours or days to minutes for tasks in materials discovery, nanotechnology, biotechnology, and applied physics.

The development places India at the forefront of agentic AI in experimental science, shifting AI from data analysis to active decision-making in real-world research environments. AILA doesn’t just process experimental data—it decides what experiments to run, monitors progress, evaluates results, and determines next steps.

In healthcare, Nature published research on AI-based agents for chronic liver disease, demonstrating how agents can transform diagnostic and therapeutic workflows through clinical decision-making. The research shows agents can handle the complexity of multi-factor diseases while maintaining explainability.

Nvidia announced a major shift toward AI agents in radiology, with new autonomous imaging systems emerging. The company also introduced NitroGen, an effort to create universal AI agents for all virtual worlds, extending agent capabilities beyond specific domains.

Multi-Agent Systems: More Isn’t Always Better¶

Research from Google and MIT found that adding more agents can lead to worse results—a “multi-agent paradox” that complicates enterprise deployment. The study identified diminishing returns as agent count increases, with coordination overhead and error propagation offsetting the benefits of parallel processing.

The findings have practical implications. While multi-agent systems promise specialization and collaboration, poorly designed architectures can perform worse than single agents. The research suggests enterprises should carefully evaluate whether multi-agent approaches are justified for specific use cases rather than assuming more agents equals better performance.

Coding, Memory, and Other Advances¶

Ars Technica tested four AI coding agents by tasking them with rebuilding Minesweeper, with explosive results—literally and figuratively. The test revealed significant variability in agent capabilities, with some agents producing working code quickly while others struggled with basic implementation details.

Vectorize achieved a breakthrough on LongMemEval with an open-source AI agent memory system, addressing a key limitation in agent architectures. Memory systems enable agents to maintain context and learn from past interactions, essential for long-running autonomous workflows.

Fujitsu outlined its approach to revolutionizing AI agent collaboration, memory, and quality, focusing on enterprise-grade reliability. The company emphasized that production agents need robust collaboration protocols and persistent memory to handle real business workflows.

Enterprise Adoption: Promise Meets Reality¶

The gap between AI agent ambition and enterprise reality remains substantial. A study by theCUBE Research found that while 62% of companies see agents as key for decision-making, trust scores remain low and operational readiness lags behind investment. Trust is the critical factor determining ROI.

Boston Consulting Group warned that incidents from autonomous AI agents climbed 21% from 2024 to 2025, raising fresh enterprise risks. The increase comes as companies deploy more agents without adequate governance, creating vulnerabilities around data exposure, unauthorized actions, and compliance failures.

Industry Opportunities¶

Financial services present a $6.54 billion market by 2035 according to Precedence Research, growing from $1.79 billion in 2025 at a 13.84% CAGR. LSEG explored how agents power financial workflows, moving from simple automation to autonomous decision-making.

Advertising and marketing adoption is immediate and growing. GenAI app spending hit $824 million as marketers adopt AI agents according to industry analysis. LINE Yahoo deployed advertising agents, and IAS launched an AI agent for ad campaign optimization.

Real-World Deployments¶

The German Federal Employment Agency automated Jira processes with AI agents, demonstrating practical application in government operations.

Salesforce is upgrading its AI agents from “interns” to experienced employees, pushing a unified data model for safer agents. The company also acquired Qualified in an agentic marketing push, integrating the company’s technology into its agent platform.

SoftBank reported creating 2.5 million agents company-wide, while Prosus is building an internal workforce of 30,000 AI agents by March. Sompo announced plans to deploy AI agents across 30,000 employees, and Concentrix launched emotionally aware conversational AI agents for brand experience.

Novel Energy Solutions is using AI agents from Procore to automate engineering tasks for solar farm development, improving design accuracy and accelerating project timelines. Zepto Cafe tested an AI agent that places orders for users, and CaterAi launched an AI agent for event catering planning.

Security and Governance: The Infrastructure Gap¶

Security Risks Emerge¶

Research from SquareX found that browser AI agents are proving riskier than human employees for certain attack vectors. Unlike human employees, browser agents aren’t subject to security awareness training and can’t recognize visual warning signs of phishing attacks. The research focused on OAuth attacks and noted that agents’ systematic nature makes them vulnerable to exploitation.

Tenable demonstrated how to hack Microsoft’s Copilot AI agent, exposing fraud risks in agentic systems. The hack showed how agents could be manipulated into unauthorized actions, highlighting the need for robust guardrails.

Okta reported that AI agent security is the top customer concern, with enterprises worried about data exposure, unauthorized actions, and compliance violations. SC Media noted that AI agent adoption will expose infrastructure and security gaps in 2026 as companies deploy agents without adequate preparation.

Governance Solutions Emerge¶

OWASP released the Top 10 for Agentic Applications (2026), identifying the highest-impact security risks in autonomous, tool-using AI agent systems. The list addresses novel attack surfaces like Agent Goal Hijack and Tool Misuse that traditional application security models don’t cover. Aikido Security contributed research to the effort, noting these risks are already emerging in real systems.

Veza introduced AI Agent Security, a product to govern and protect agentic AI at enterprise scale. The company extended its platform to address agent-specific risks, focusing on identity and privilege management.

Superagent launched an open-source framework for guardrails around agentic AI, providing developers with tools to implement safety constraints. Human Security enhanced AI agent security through Amazon Bedrock AgentCore integration.

For banking, American Banker argued that “Know Your Customer” is evolving into “Know Your Agent,” as financial institutions need to understand not just their customers but also the agents acting on their behalf. Fox Business reported on the rise of “rogue” AI agents pushing companies to lock down data.

Consent and Privacy Questions¶

Microsoft’s policy change on Windows 11 AI agent access to personal files sparked user outrage, leading the company to confirm that consent will be required. The incident raised broader questions about agent access to sensitive data.

Tech Policy Press explored the question of when an AI agent says “I agree,” who’s consenting—the user or the agent? As agents make more autonomous decisions, the legal frameworks around consent and authorization will need clarification.

VentureBeat warned that agent autonomy without guardrails creates site reliability engineering nightmares, as unpredictable agent behavior can cascade into system-wide failures.

Looking Ahead: What 2026 Holds for AI Agents¶

The $2 billion Manus acquisition validates the agentic AI market but also accelerates competitive pressure. Standardization efforts through the Agentic AI Foundation suggest the industry recognizes fragmentation is slowing adoption. Platform announcements from all major players mean 2026 will be about execution, not announcements.

Research shows agents can match human performance in specific domains—ARTEMIS outperforming human penetration testers, AILA conducting real experiments, Oxford’s multi-agent cancer care assistant—but also reveals real limitations. The multi-agent paradox research from Google and MIT demonstrates that more agents don’t automatically mean better results. Microsoft’s selling challenges, with agents failing real-world tasks at high rates, underscore the gap between demos and production.

Security concerns are the gating factor for enterprise adoption. SquareX research showing browser AI agents are riskier than human employees, Tenable’s Copilot hack exposing fraud risks, and BCG’s warning of a 21% increase in incidents all point to the same reality: security infrastructure hasn’t caught up to agent capabilities.

The key question for 2026: can agents move from impressive demos to reliable, secure, production-ready systems? Progress is real but so are the challenges. Enterprises need agents that work consistently within existing workflows, not standalone tools that require rethinking entire processes. They need governance frameworks that prevent unauthorized actions while preserving autonomy. They need security models that address agent-specific risks like goal hijacking and tool misuse.

The year ahead will likely see increased focus on agent reliability, measurement, and governance. Companies that can demonstrate production-grade performance with robust security will pull ahead. Those that continue shipping demos masquerading as products will face enterprise pushback.

Agentic AI is moving from experimental to essential, but the path forward requires solving real problems in reliability and security, not adding more features. The $2 billion bet on Manus suggests the market believes those problems are solvable. 2026 will test that belief.
