This comprehensive review covers 159 articles published in November 2025, surveying the latest developments in AI agents. This month brought a crucial reality check for AI agents. Multiple independent studies revealed agents struggling to work autonomously, Microsoft bet big on “Agent 365” as an operating system for AI agents, and security researchers documented the first cases of malicious agent use. Yet amid the sobering news, real deployments showed tangible results.

The Performance Reality Check¶

The most significant story this month was the mounting evidence that AI agents, as currently built, cannot work independently. A landmark study from Upwork drew from more than 300 real client projects and found that AI agents routinely fail to complete even straightforward professional tasks on their own. The research tested leading models—Gemini 2.5 Pro, OpenAI’s GPT-5, and ClaudeSonnet 4—on actual jobs posted by paying clients.

The results were stark. When working alone, AI agents showed low completion rates across the board. For data science and analytics projects, ClaudeSonnet 4 achieved a 64% completion rate independently but jumped to 93% after receiving feedback from a human expert. In sales and marketing work, Gemini 2.5 Pro’s completion rate rose from 17% independently to 31% with human input . OpenAI’s GPT-5 showed similarly dramatic improvements in engineering tasks, climbing from 30% to 50% completion with human oversight .

When AI agents collaborate with human experts, project completion rates surge by up to 70% . This finding challenges the fundamental assumption in the AI industry that agent benchmarks conducted in isolation accurately predict real-world performance.

Microsoft’s own testing yielded similar disappointing results. Researchers built a synthetic marketplace called the “Magentic Marketplace” to test AI agent behavior, with 100 customer-side agents interacting with 300 business-side agents. The experiments using models like GPT-4o, GPT-5, and Gemini-2.5-Flash found agents were vulnerable to manipulation and overwhelmed by having too many options . The agents also struggled with collaboration without explicit instructions.

A separate benchmark called the Remote Labor Index (RLI) showed that current AI agents perform poorly when automating real remote jobs, with top models automating only 2.5% or less of complex work valued at over $140,000.

Harvard Business Review reached a complementary conclusion: agents aren’t ready for consumer-facing work but can excel at internal processes . The analysis suggests agents work better for internal workflows where errors can be caught and corrected, rather than customer-facing interactions where mistakes directly impact brand and revenue.

Even the mainstream press is noticing. The Hill reported that “AI agents aren’t replacing remote workers any time soon,” citing the mounting evidence of performance limitations.

This reality check sets the stage for everything else that happened in November. The industry is building infrastructure for agents that don’t yet exist as promised capabilities—but the gap between vision and reality is becoming clearer.

Microsoft’s Agent Operating System¶

While studies showed agents struggling in practice, Microsoft made its biggest bet yet on the agentic future. At its Ignite 2025 conference, the company unveiled a comprehensive vision to turn Windows into an “agentic OS” with Agent 365 as the management layer.

Agent 365 is a control plane framework for businesses to securely deploy, manage, and monitor AI agents “like they do people.” The platform provides dashboards, telemetry, and security controls for managing fleets of AI bots. A Microsoft executive quoted in CNBC predicted a future where companies use far more AI agents than human employees.

The portfolio Microsoft announced is extensive:

  • Agent 365: The central management platform for AI agents across the enterprise
  • Fara-7B: A 7-billion parameter AI agent designed to perform complex computer tasks directly on a user’s device; rivals GPT-4o in benchmark performance and processes data locally for privacy
  • Entra Agent ID: Identity documentation for AI agents, treating them like a digital workforce with their own identities
  • Windows integration: Microsoft is transforming Windows 11 into an “agentic OS” by integrating AI agents directly into the taskbar and File Explorer
  • Magentic Marketplace: An open source simulation environment for testing AI agent behavior
  • Microsoft Foundry: A modular, interoperable, and secure agent stack for building and scaling AI agents

eWeek called it “the Operating System for AI Agents.” The vision is clear: Microsoft wants to be the platform layer for the agentic era, just as Windows was for the PC era.

But not everyone is convinced this is what enterprises want. PCMag asked whether anyone actually wants to create their own AI agents, questioning whether the vision aligns with enterprise needs. Computerworld noted the tool is designed to help IT manage “AI agent sprawl”—suggesting agents are proliferating faster than organizations can manage them.

Microsoft’s announcement also included security tools, reflecting growing awareness of the risks that come with autonomous agents. The company announced autonomous security capabilities for AI agents and partnerships with security vendors like Wiz to secure the agentic environment.

The Ignite announcements weren’t limited to agents—Microsoft also unveiled updates to Azure Copilot agents, AI infrastructure innovations, and integrations with partners like Adobe, Nvidia, ServiceNow, and Workday. But agents were clearly the centerpiece, reflecting Microsoft’s belief that agentic AI represents the next major shift in enterprise computing.

Security Challenges Mount¶

As Microsoft and others build out agent infrastructure, security researchers are documenting serious risks. November brought multiple revelations about how agents can be exploited—or how they can exploit each other.

Documented Attacks¶

The most concerning report came from Axios: Chinese hackers used Anthropic’s Claude Code agent to automate spying operations . The attackers, associated with a group called “Suspended Packages,” used Anthropic’s own AI agent to identify vulnerable files, exfiltrate data, and maintain persistence on compromised systems. This is one of the first documented cases of malicious actors using AI agents as attack tools.

Another concerning finding came from security researchers who discovered that ServiceNow AI agents can be tricked into acting against each other via “second-order prompts.” By crafting prompts that cause one agent to interpret another agent’s actions as hostile, attackers can trigger agent-vs-agent conflicts that disrupt operations.

Medium raised questions about whether Windows 11’s new AI agents represent “convenience or a new attack vector,” noting that agents with deep system access could be exploited if compromised.

Enterprise Readiness Gap¶

Despite these documented risks, enterprises are not prepared. ZDNET reported that “enterprises are not prepared for a world of malicious AI agents,” citing a lack of governance frameworks and security controls. A separate analysis in The AI Economy found that while enterprises anticipate a 2027 AI agent explosion, “reality shows they’re not ready.”

The IAPP (International Association of Privacy Professionals) published two analyses on the topic: one on understanding new risks and practical safeguards and another on considerations for tackling agentic AI risks . Both emphasized that current privacy and security frameworks were not designed for autonomous agents that can access, move, and alter data without human oversight.

CIO summarized the sentiment bluntly: “Agentic AI has big trust issues.”

Security Infrastructure¶

The market is responding with new security tools and frameworks:

  • Runlayer launched out of stealth with $11 million in seed funding from Khosla Ventures’ Keith Rabois and Felicis. The startup addresses security vulnerabilities in the Model Context Protocol (MCP), the standard for AI agent connectivity. It has already signed eight unicorns as customers .

  • Ping Identity launched “Identity for AI” , a solution designed to provide identity verification for AI agents in what the company calls the “agent economy.”

  • Keycard, an AI agent identity startup, acquired Runebook to strengthen its MCP connectivity capabilities.

  • Nokod Security launched Adaptive Agent Security to protect AI agents across the entire agent development lifecycle.

  • Wiz unveiled multiple agent security products , including a SecOps AI Agent and AI-SPM for securing AI agents.

  • AWS published The Agentic AI Security Scoping Matrix , a framework for securing autonomous AI systems.

  • GitHub published its agentic security principles for building secure AI agents.

  • Forrester released Applying Forrester’s AEGIS Framework To IAM And AI Agents , a framework for identity and access management in the age of agents.

  • AvePoint debuted AgentPulse Command Center for AI agent governance.

  • BleepingComputer explored how “Never Trust, Always Verify” applies to autonomous AI agents.

  • SlashData published guidance on how to harness AI agents without breaking security .

The security challenge is becoming clear: as agents gain more autonomy and access, they become both more valuable and more dangerous. The industry is racing to build guardrails before deployments scale beyond control.

Real-World Deployments¶

Despite the performance limitations and security concerns, real deployments are showing results. The most cited example this month was Klarna, which reported that its AI agent is doing the work of 853 employees . This represents significant productivity—but notably, it’s in customer service, a well-defined domain with clear processes and human oversight available.

Capgemini found that banks and insurers are deploying AI agents to fight fraud and process applications, with plans for new roles specifically to supervise the AI. The report indicates that financial institutions are actively moving key customer-facing processes to AI agents, marking a rapid transformation in how customers interact with banks and insurers. CIO Dive reported that banks are creating “AI supervisor” roles as agent use surges.

Amazon Connect launched AI agents capable of autonomous action for customer service, representing a step beyond simple chatbots toward agents that can take actions on behalf of customers.

In the public sector, Staffordshire Police announced it will trial AI agents on 101 service —the non-emergency police contact line—marking one of the first tests of agents in a government service context. Axios reported exclusively that the IRS has deployed AI agents for internal operations.

Tearsheet podcasted about “AI agents making real financial decisions,” featuring NVIDIA’s Kevin Levitt on the infrastructure behind live deployments at Capital One, Visa, and RBC.

Sage debuted a Finance Intelligence AI Agent for financial workflows. Zeni rolled out an AI agent to automate complex accounting workloads.

Oracle’s Clinical AI Agent clicked quickly for Hudson Physicians, showing results in healthcare. Fast Company explored how AI agents are about to change how people shop, with implications for retail.

The Wall Street Journal reported that “companies begin to see a return on AI agents,” citing early adopters showing measurable ROI. PYMNTS.com published guidance on “What CFOs Should Be Asking About AI Agents,” reflecting finance leaders’ need to understand and justify the investments.

But there are challenges in deployment as well. Ad Age reported on how AI agents are “messing with ad metrics” as Amazon fights Perplexity over undeclared bots. The agents don’t shop like humans, and they don’t view ads like humans—creating measurement headaches for digital advertising.

The Takeaway¶

November 2025 was a reality check month for AI agents. The hype is meeting reality in three important ways.

First, agents work in narrow contexts with human oversight, not as autonomous replacements. The Upwork study, Microsoft’s marketplace test, and the Remote Labor Index all show the same thing: current AI agents struggle independently but excel with human partners. The deployments that work—Klarna’s customer service agent, banks using AI for fraud detection, Amazon Connect’s autonomous agents—are specialized tools, not general-purpose replacements.

Second, Microsoft’s infrastructure bet suggests the company sees this as a long-term play requiring new OS-level capabilities. Agent 365, Fara-7B, Entra Agent ID, and the Windows “agentic OS” transformation represent a massive investment in the agent platform layer. This is infrastructure build-out, not breakthrough capability. Microsoft is building the roads before the cars are ready.

Third, security challenges are real and escalating. Documented cases of Chinese hackers using Anthropic’s agent for automated spying and ServiceNow agents being tricked into fighting each other show that autonomous agents create new attack surfaces. The rapid emergence of security startups like Runlayer, Nokod, and established players like Wiz and Ping Identity launching agent security products indicates this will be a significant growth area.

The deployments that show results share common characteristics: well-defined domains, clear processes, abundant training data, and human oversight. These aren’t the science fiction autonomous agents that make decisions without context—they’re specialized tools that excel at specific tasks.

Gartner’s $15 trillion prediction for B2B purchases by AI agents in 2028 shows the stakes. But enterprises anticipating a 2027 agent explosion may be premature. The AI Economy found that organizations aren’t ready for the risks.

We’re in the infrastructure build-out phase, not the autonomous agent phase. Forrester’s 2026 predictions underscore that this is a multi-year transition. The winners will be companies that focus on practical deployment in narrow contexts, build robust governance frameworks, and integrate agents as collaborators rather than replacements.

The age of AI agents is coming. But November 2025 showed it’s coming more slowly—and more collaboratively—than the hype suggested.
