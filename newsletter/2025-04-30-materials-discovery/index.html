This month, researchers deployed transformer models to predict alloy properties, LLMs to design molecules, and autonomous labs to accelerate discovery. Meanwhile, a surprising finding revealed billions in rare earth elements hiding in coal ash waste.

This comprehensive review covers 18 articles published in April 2025, surveying the latest advances at the intersection of AI and materials discovery.

The Big Story: AI Models Scale Materials Discovery¶

Transformer architectures, originally developed for natural language processing, are now demonstrating their value in materials science. Researchers at the University of North Texas applied a transformer-based language model to predict mechanical properties of high-entropy alloys (HEAs). The approach addresses the perennial challenge of data scarcity in materials science through pre-training on synthetic data followed by fine-tuning on experimental HEA datasets. The model outperformed traditional regression methods including random forests, Gaussian processes, gradient boosting, support vector machines, k-nearest neighbors, artificial neural networks, deep neural networks, and convolutional neural networks.

The transformer architecture offers an additional advantage: interpretability through attention weight visualization, allowing researchers to understand which features drive predictions. This transparency is valuable for materials scientists who need to understand the physical basis of model predictions.

MIT researchers introduced Llamole, a multimodal AI system that combines large language models with graph-based molecular representations for synthesis planning . The approach improved synthesis planning success rates from 5% to 35% compared to baseline methods. The system uses contrastive learning to align molecular graphs with text descriptions, enabling the LLM to reason about chemical structures more effectively. While 35% may seem modest, it represents a seven-fold improvement over conventional approaches.

Two papers in Science on April 4 highlighted complementary AI-driven approaches. Researchers at the University of Toronto demonstrated that active learning combined with domain adaptation accelerates the discovery of advanced oxygen evolution electrocatalysts for water splitting. By strategically selecting experiments and adapting models across related domains, the approach reduces the experimental burden of discovering efficient electrocatalysts.

Separately, a high-throughput experimentation platform published in Science Advances enables rapid screening and optimization of electrochemical processes. The platform facilitates accelerated materials discovery through automated, data-driven methods that can handle large numbers of experiments in parallel.

For organic photovoltaics, researchers developed a machine learning framework using polymer-unit fingerprints (PUFp) with a random forest algorithm. The PUFp representation outperformed traditional MACCS fingerprints for predicting power conversion efficiency. The database includes 1,343 experimentally validated OPV non-fullerene acceptor materials and 260 donor materials, providing a substantial foundation for training predictive models. The PUFp system uses 413 different polymer units for N-type OPVs and 209 for P-type OPVs, compared to the 166 digital keys in MACCS fingerprints.

Self-Driving Labs: From Vision to Infrastructure¶

Self-driving laboratories (SDLs) are transitioning from conceptual frameworks to operational research infrastructure. A perspective article in Nature articulates two distinct paradigms for democratizing SDL-assisted research: centralized facilities and distributed networks of open-source tools.

The centralized model, exemplified by facilities like CERN, provides access to sophisticated equipment that individual labs could not afford. The distributed model, drawing inspiration from projects like Galaxy Zoo and Foldit, leverages networks of researchers and open-source tools to spread access more broadly. The article highlights several enabling technologies including Chemspyd, PyLabRobot, PerQueue, and Jubilee that provide building blocks for constructing autonomous laboratory systems.

According to the perspective, SDLs address two critical bottlenecks in materials discovery: the pace of experimentation and the accessibility of advanced equipment. By automating experiment design and execution, these systems can tackle grand challenges that would be impractical for human-operated labs.

Concrete examples of autonomous labs in action are emerging in photonics research. An article in Optics & Photonics News describes real-world implementations of self-driving labs for optical materials discovery. These systems combine automated synthesis platforms with in-line characterization and closed-loop optimization, enabling researchers to explore materials spaces more thoroughly than manual methods allow.

The democratization angle is significant. While high-throughput facilities have existed for decades, the emergence of modular, open-source SDL components could allow smaller research groups to build autonomous capabilities tailored to their specific needs. This could broaden participation in advanced materials research beyond well-funded institutions.

Centralized vs. Distributed Models¶

The Nature perspective distinguishes between facility-based SDLs, which concentrate resources and expertise, and distributed networks that leverage collaboration and shared software. Each approach has trade-offs: centralized facilities offer specialized equipment and staff support, while distributed models provide broader access and flexibility.

Real-World Implementations¶

The Optics & Photonics article highlights working SDLs that demonstrate the practical benefits of autonomous experimentation, including accelerated discovery cycles and the ability to run experiments continuously without human intervention.

Industrial Adoption and Infrastructure¶

Commercialization and infrastructure investments accelerated in April, signaling growing industry confidence in AI-driven materials discovery approaches.

Product Launch: SES AI’s Molecular Universe¶

SES AI Corporation launched Molecular Universe MU-0, a battery material discovery platform powered by physics and AI. The platform features an interactive map of small molecules embedded in a 512-dimensional space using UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection). This high-dimensional representation captures complex molecular relationships that can guide the discovery of new battery materials.

The platform has already attracted two signed OEM customers, according to SES AI Founder and CEO Qichao Hu . A live demo was scheduled for April 29, 2025. Founded in 2012, SES AI is positioning MU-0 as a tool to accelerate next-generation battery development for both Li-Metal and Li-ion chemistries.

Partnership: Microsoft and Johns Hopkins¶

Microsoft and the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory entered a strategic collaboration to advance robotics and materials discovery using AI. The partnership focuses on two areas: developing autonomous robot teaming systems with generative AI for disaster and battlefield scenarios, and testing Microsoft’s MatterGen model for materials discovery aimed at extreme environments.

The collaboration aims to enhance capabilities for scenarios where human presence is risky or impractical, combining APL’s domain expertise with Microsoft’s AI infrastructure. MatterGen, Microsoft’s generative model for materials design, will be tested on materials discovery problems relevant to extreme environment applications.

Infrastructure: UK Semiconductor Investment¶

The University of Sheffield received £7 million to establish an AI-driven molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) system for semiconductor materials discovery. The system will use AI to control crystal growth at the atomic level, enabling the discovery of new semiconductor materials with tailored properties.

This investment represents a national capability for the UK, providing researchers with access to advanced synthesis equipment enhanced by AI control. The MBE approach allows precise deposition of materials layer by layer, and AI optimization could accelerate the discovery of materials for quantum computing, photonics, and other advanced applications.

Unexpected Discoveries¶

Billions in Rare Earths Found in Coal Ash¶

Researchers led by the University of Texas at Austin discovered that coal ash waste contains significant quantities of rare earth elements. The study estimates 11 million tons of rare earth elements in U.S. coal ash, compared to 1.4 million tons in conventional sources. At current market values, this represents approximately $8.4 billion worth of critical materials.

The finding challenges the conventional view of coal ash as purely waste material. According to study authors Davin Bagdonas and Bridget Scanlon , the widespread availability of coal ash across the U.S. could reduce dependency on foreign sources, which currently dominate the market. The Asia Pacific region accounted for 86.14% of the rare earth market in 2023.

Chris Young , chief strategy officer at ElementUSA, noted that extracting rare earths from coal ash could have lower environmental impact than conventional mining. The global rare earth market was valued at $3.74 billion in 2024 and is projected to reach $8.14 billion by 2032, driven by demand for clean technologies.

Thermodynamics-Defying Materials for EV Restoration¶

University of Chicago scientists discovered materials that appear to defy conventional thermodynamics, exhibiting properties that could enable the restoration of degraded EV batteries. The materials, which include metastable compounds with negative thermal expansion, maintain their structure under conditions that would typically cause decomposition.

These anomalous materials challenge the understanding of stability in solid-state systems and could provide new pathways for battery recycling and rejuvenation. The research suggests that certain materials can exist in states that thermodynamic calculations would predict to be unstable, opening new possibilities for energy storage applications.

Car Exhaust Improves Catalyst Performance¶

In a serendipitous discovery at Washington State University , researchers found that exposing certain catalysts to car exhaust gases actually improved their performance. The unexpected result emerged during experiments designed to study catalyst degradation under realistic conditions.

The researchers discovered that specific components of exhaust, including carbon monoxide and hydrocarbons, can modify catalyst surfaces in ways that enhance activity for certain reactions. This counterintuitive finding could lead to new catalyst treatment methods that improve efficiency for emission control and chemical synthesis applications.

Hidden Quantum States in Twisted Materials¶

A Japan-US collaboration revealed hidden quantum states in twisted bilayer materials that were not predicted by conventional theory. The discovery involved creating previously unobserved twist angle configurations and measuring their electronic properties.

The exotic states emerge from the moiré patterns created when two-dimensional materials are twisted relative to each other. These findings expand the understanding of quantum phenomena in layered materials and could inform the design of materials for quantum computing and advanced electronics.

Hydrogel Reprograms Cancer Cells¶

Researchers at Hokkaido University discovered that a specific double-network hydrogel can rapidly reprogram fully differentiated cancer cells into cancer stem cells (CSCs) through a process they call HARP (hydrogel-activated reprogramming phenomenon). Within 24 hours, cancer cells form CSC spheres on the hydrogel surface.

Cancer stem cells are typically extremely rare, occurring at approximately 1 in a million cells in a tumor, making them difficult to study. The ability to generate large quantities of CSCs in the lab accelerates research into therapies that target these treatment-resistant cells, which are believed to drive cancer recurrence. According to Professor Shinya Tanaka , this approach could enable personalized medicine approaches and novel drug screening platforms.

Looking Ahead¶

The convergence of several trends suggests that AI-driven materials discovery is moving from experimental approach to established practice. Nature Computational Science launched a collection on generative models for molecular design and discovery, signaling the field’s maturation. The collection highlights approaches including variational autoencoders, diffusion models, and large language models for navigating the vast theoretical chemical space, which contains an estimated \(10^{60}\) feasible compounds.

Kristin Persson , founder of the Materials Project, noted that the combination of high-quality data and machine learning is transforming how researchers approach materials discovery. In an interview with the Department of Energy, she emphasized that data-driven methods allow researchers to explore chemical spaces more systematically than traditional trial-and-error approaches.

The direction of travel points toward solving major challenges. A Scientific American analysis examined how new materials could ease climate impacts, highlighting the role of AI in accelerating the discovery of materials for clean energy, carbon capture, and sustainable infrastructure.

The pattern is clear: AI architectures are scaling to handle more complex materials problems, self-driving labs are democratizing access to automated experimentation, and industry is investing in infrastructure to bring these advances from lab to market. The unexpected discoveries—from rare earths in coal ash to thermodynamics-defying materials—remind us that materials science still holds the capacity for surprise, even as computational methods make exploration more systematic.
