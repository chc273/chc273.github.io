In a single afternoon, researchers discovered a catalyst that replaces expensive iridium for clean hydrogen production. This month, $120 million in funding poured into AI materials startups. Autonomous labs now perform 1,000 experiments daily. The era of slow, accidental materials discovery is ending.

This comprehensive review covers 29 articles published during August 2025, surveying the latest developments in AI-accelerated materials science. High-throughput methods are achieving 10-1000x speed improvements over traditional approaches, autonomous laboratories are moving from research prototypes to commercial facilities, and foundation models trained on billions of molecules are emerging as powerful new tools for materials design.

The Breakthrough¶

On August 19, researchers at Northwestern University, in collaboration with the Toyota Research Institute, announced they had discovered a new catalyst for clean hydrogen production—in a single afternoon. The catalyst, made from a combination of four abundant metals, matches or exceeds the performance of iridium for the oxygen evolution reaction while costing just one-sixteenth as much.

This was no accident. The team used a nanomaterial “megalibrary” containing 156 million distinct particles on a single chip. Using machine learning algorithms to analyze the results, they identified a Ru52Co33Mn9Cr6 oxide catalyst that remained stable for 1,000 hours of testing. Iridium currently costs approximately $5,000 per ounce, creating a major barrier to scaling green hydrogen production.

The megalibrary platform, first introduced in 2016, represents a shift from serendipitous discovery to systematic exploration. Rather than testing materials one by one, researchers can now screen millions of compositions in parallel. The Northwestern discovery demonstrates what this approach makes possible: a practical problem (replacing rare iridium) solved in an afternoon instead of years of trial and error.

This speed is becoming characteristic of the field. Professor Toshiaki Taniike at the Japan Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (JAIST) reports that high-throughput experiments combined with machine learning are 10 to 1000 times faster than traditional manual methods. His work on catalysts and polymers uses automatic feature engineering and data science to accelerate discovery, aiming to unlock new chemical reactions like the oxidative coupling of methane for carbon-neutral processes.

The pattern is consistent: materials scientists who once spent months testing hypotheses can now screen thousands of compositions per day, with AI identifying the most promising candidates for human validation.

The Engine: How AI Accelerates Discovery¶

The acceleration of materials discovery isn’t happening by accident. It’s driven by a convergence of technologies: autonomous laboratories that run experiments continuously, AI foundation models trained on billions of molecules, and closed-loop systems where AI plans experiments and learns from the results in real time.

Autonomous Laboratories¶

The most visible sign of this transformation is the emergence of self-driving laboratories. On August 25, researchers at North Carolina State University unveiled Rainbow, a multi-robot autonomous laboratory that can perform 1,000 experiments per day and process 96 reactions simultaneously. Rainbow uses AI and robotics to autonomously synthesize and optimize quantum dots for applications in displays, solar cells, and LEDs.

Rainbow is a research platform, but commercial autonomous labs are already operational. Chemify, a spin-out from the University of Glasgow, has opened its first Chemifarm facility—a 21,500 square foot automated lab in Glasgow that uses a chemical programming language and robotics to accelerate drug and materials discovery, according to a report in Technology Networks . The facility represents the first commercial implementation of the “chemputation” platform, which founder Leroy Cronin envisions enabling decentralized manufacturing to address supply chain vulnerabilities. Chemify has grown to 140 employees in just 3.5 years.

Government labs are also open-sourcing their autonomous platforms. NIST announced the Autonomous Formulation Lab (AFL) on August 29, an open-source platform that uses AI-driven robotics to autonomously prepare and test complex fluid mixtures. The system links AI planning with robotic execution and in-situ measurements like small-angle scattering to map phase behavior and optimize formulations for products like paints and vaccines. NIST has made both the code and hardware designs publicly available.

AI Foundation Models¶

While robotics accelerate the physical work of experimentation, AI foundation models are accelerating the intellectual work of prediction and design. Argonne National Laboratory is using supercomputers to build AI foundation models trained on billions of molecules—a dramatic scale increase from previous models trained on 100 million molecules. The team trained one of the largest chemical foundation models on the Polaris supercomputer in 2024 and is now developing a second model for molecular crystals on the Aurora exascale system.

These foundation models are integrated with chatbots to enable interactive exploration and have already outperformed previous single-property prediction models. The goal is to predict properties for new battery materials including electrolytes and electrodes. Most battery materials in use today were discovered between 1975 and 1985; foundation models could accelerate the discovery of their successors.

At Tohoku University, researchers have developed an AI-built “materials map” using the MatDeepLearn (MDL) framework and message passing neural networks. The map unifies experimental and computational data to visually group structurally similar materials, allowing scientists to quickly identify promising candidates and repurpose existing synthesis protocols. The team demonstrated the approach for thermoelectric materials and plans to extend it to magnetic and topological materials.

Closed-Loop Discovery¶

The real breakthrough comes when AI planning, robotic execution, and real-time learning are integrated into closed-loop systems. NIST’s AFL exemplifies this approach: AI plans experiments, robots execute them, in-situ measurements provide immediate feedback, and the AI uses those results to plan the next round of experiments. Chemify’s Chemifarm operates on similar principles, with the goal of making chemical discovery and manufacturing a closed-loop, automated process.

Quantum-Integrated Platforms¶

A new class of platforms is adding quantum computing to the discovery toolkit. On August 19, Mitsui, QSimulate, and Quantinuum launched the QIDO platform , a quantum-integrated chemistry platform that combines classical and quantum computing workflows for high-precision chemical reaction modeling. QIDO integrates QSimulate’s QSP Reaction software with Quantinuum’s InQuanto software, making quantum chemistry technologies more accessible to industrial settings.

What They’re Finding¶

The accelerated discovery methods are producing results across multiple domains. New materials are emerging for energy storage, electronics, and specialty applications.

Energy Storage¶

Battery materials research has seen particularly rapid progress. Researchers using a chemical foundation model called SMI-TED have designed new electrolyte formulations for rechargeable batteries. The model, fine-tuned on 13,666 experimental data points, discovered 7 novel formulations that improved ionic conductivity for LiFSI- and LiDFOB-based electrolytes by 82% and 172%, respectively. The workflow is generalizable beyond electrolytes to other chemical mixtures.

At Argonne National Laboratory, foundation models trained on billions of molecules are being applied to discover new materials for both electrolytes and electrodes. Meanwhile, an analysis of the AI-driven battery technology market projects the sector will grow to $19.4 billion by 2034, with a compound annual growth rate of 18.9%. The report notes that AI has identified 500,000 potential new battery materials at Microsoft alone.

Separate research covered by SciTechDaily highlights AI’s role in discovering alternatives to lithium-ion chemistry, including sodium-based and solid-state battery technologies.

Electronics¶

Next-generation power electronics are another focus of accelerated discovery. Researchers have used high-throughput computational screening to identify new materials for power electronics, screening 153,235 materials from the Materials Project database and identifying 44 promising candidates including B2O3, BeO, and BN. The power electronics market is projected to reach $50 billion by 2025, and approximately 80% of electric power uses power electronics.

Separate computational screening identified novel ultra-wide bandgap semiconductors, with materials like InBO3 showing performance over 6,000 times better than silicon for RF electronics applications. Researchers screened 1,300 potential semiconductor materials using Density Functional Theory and Monte Carlo simulations. These materials could enable more efficient electric vehicle chargers, wireless power transfer, radar systems, and communication devices.

For two-dimensional materials, researchers have developed a substrate-aware computational design method that predicts atomic structure on substrates, combining an evolutionary algorithm, machine-learning interatomic potentials, and lattice-matching. The method discovered new stable and metastable structures in the molybdenum-sulfur system on sapphire substrates and predicts synthesis conditions using ab initio thermodynamics. The Computational 2D Materials Database contains 16,000 entries, and 2D materials are expected to enable transistor scaling down to ~1 nm by 2031.

Specialty Materials¶

Data-driven approaches are also advancing specialty materials. Researchers have developed super-adhesive hydrogels for underwater environments by mining protein databases and optimizing formulations from 180 bioinspired hydrogels. The resulting materials achieved adhesive strengths exceeding 1 MPa. The team analyzed a dataset of 24,707 adhesive proteins from 3,822 organisms, selecting the top 200 species for analysis and deriving 180 unique compositions for hydrogel synthesis.

The Northwestern megalibrary used to discover the iridium replacement catalyst has also proven effective for discovering quantum dots and other nanomaterials. Rainbow, the multi-robot laboratory at NC State, is specifically designed to accelerate quantum dot discovery and optimization.

Market Validation: $120M Bets on AI Materials¶

Capital is flowing into AI materials discovery, signaling confidence that the technology is moving from lab to market.

  • NSF and Intel are investing $20 million over five years to establish the Artificial Intelligence Materials Institute at Cornell University. The institute aims to accelerate materials discovery for sustainable energy and quantum technologies by integrating AI with materials science. Partner institutions include Princeton University, City College of the City University of New York, the Advanced Science Research Center at CUNY, and Boston University. Eun-Ah Kim will serve as director.

  • CuspAI, a British startup, is in talks to raise over $100 million to use artificial intelligence models for discovering new materials. Venture capital firm NEA is set to co-lead the funding round. The investment aims to accelerate AI-driven material discovery for applications including energy and electronics.

  • The broader AI in materials discovery market is showing strong growth. Precedence Research projects significant expansion as industries adopt AI-powered tools for materials design and discovery.

  • Princeton University was tapped as a partner in the new NSF-funded AI materials institute announced in July, further expanding the national network of research institutions focused on AI-accelerated materials science.

Reality Check: AI’s Scientific Limitations¶

Despite the progress, AI models have significant limitations in scientific reasoning. A new benchmark called MaCBench, introduced in Nature Machine Intelligence on August 11, reveals that even the best multimodal AI models struggle with spatial reasoning and cross-modal synthesis. The benchmark contains 779 multiple-choice questions and 374 numeric-answer questions covering chemistry and materials science tasks.

The results show that while models like Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Llama 3.2 90B Vision excel at basic perception—achieving 0.77 average accuracy for recognizing laboratory equipment—they perform much worse on reasoning tasks. The models achieved only 0.46 average accuracy for reasoning about laboratory scenarios and 0.53 average accuracy for extracting compositions from tables. These findings suggest that advances in training data and methods are needed before multimodal AI can serve as reliable scientific assistants.

The gap between laboratory discovery and industrial application remains another challenge. In May 2025, NREL convened the ARROWS workshop, bringing together over 50 leaders in AI, materials science, and robotics to discuss how autonomous science can bridge the “valley of death” for new materials. As reported by NREL , the goal is to create intelligent, integrated systems that produce “born-qualified” materials ready for industrial scale from the start.

Workshop organizer Steven R. Spurgeon of NREL, pioneer Sergei V. Kalinin of the University of Tennessee, Knoxville, and Nathan Park of IBM Research discussed approaches including AI reward functions, causal models, agent-based AI models, modular workflows, and standardized data platforms. The challenge isn’t just discovering materials—it’s ensuring that discovered materials can be manufactured at scale and integrated into existing industrial processes.

What This Means¶

The pattern from August’s developments is clear: AI has moved from augmenting materials scientists to partially automating discovery. The Northwestern catalyst discovered in one afternoon, Rainbow’s 1,000 daily experiments, and foundation models trained on billions of molecules all point to the same shift—from serendipitous discovery to targeted design, from intuition-driven exploration to data-driven optimization.

The $120 million in funding announcements this month suggests that investors and government agencies see the potential for commercial impact. The autonomous labs moving from research prototypes to commercial facilities at Chemify and being open-sourced by NIST indicate that the technology is maturing.

But the MaCBench results and the NREL workshop provide important perspective. AI models still struggle with spatial reasoning and complex scientific tasks. The path from laboratory discovery to industrial product remains long and uncertain. The transformation underway is real, but it’s gradual rather than revolutionary.

The most successful organizations will likely be those that combine AI acceleration with human expertise—using autonomous labs for high-throughput screening, foundation models for prediction, and human scientists for hypothesis generation, interpretation, and application knowledge. The iridium replacement discovered at Northwestern wasn’t found by AI alone; it was found by researchers who knew exactly what problem they needed to solve and had built a tool capable of solving it.

That combination—clear problem definition, AI-accelerated exploration, and scientific expertise—is what will determine whether AI materials discovery fulfills its promise.
