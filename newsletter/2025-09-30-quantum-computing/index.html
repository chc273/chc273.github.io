This comprehensive review covers 165 articles published between September 1-30, 2025, surveying a month that fundamentally altered the quantum computing landscape. Caltech built a 6,100-qubit processor (5x the previous record). PsiQuantum raised $1 billion in a single round. Nvidia made three major strategic investments in quantum companies. HSBC ran the world’s first quantum-enabled bond trading. And quantum computers are now being manufactured in standard silicon chip foundries.

The Record Breaker¶

On September 25, Caltech physicists led by Professor Manuel Endres unveiled the largest qubit array ever assembled : a 6,100-qubit neutral-atom processor that shatters the previous record of 1,180 qubits held by Atom Computing by a factor of five. The achievement, published in Nature , represents a genuine leap forward for scalable quantum computing.

The Caltech array achieves both scale and quality: qubits maintain superposition for 13 seconds with 99.98% manipulation accuracy. The system uses 12,000 optical tweezers to trap individual cesium atoms, arranged in a 1.5mm diameter field of view with 7.2 micrometer spacing between atoms. The coherence time is 10 times longer than previous arrays, and the system can continuously reload atoms at a rate of 300,000 atoms per second while maintaining quantum information.

The breakthrough matters because neutral-atom quantum computing has emerged as one of the most promising paths to large-scale quantum processors. Unlike superconducting qubits, which require expensive dilution refrigerators and complex wiring, neutral atoms can be manipulated at room temperature and reconfigured dynamically using optical tweezers. This flexibility enables “zone-based” quantum computing architectures where qubits can be shuffled between computation, memory, and measurement regions.

Researchers believe this approach can scale to a million qubits within a decade . The Caltech team achieved 99.99% imaging fidelity and 99.98% single-qubit gate fidelity, with loading probabilities exceeding 51% per site. The array uses 11,998 tweezer sites to trap and manipulate the 6,100 active qubits.

The record-breaking system demonstrates that neutral-atom quantum computing can achieve both scale and quality simultaneously—two requirements typically in tension. Previous approaches either achieved high qubit counts with poor coherence, or excellent coherence with limited qubit numbers. Caltech’s system delivers both: room-temperature trapping lifetimes of 23 minutes and coherent transport distances of 610 micrometers with 99.95% transport fidelity.

This advancement comes alongside progress from Harvard, MIT, and QuEra, who demonstrated a 3,000-qubit system capable of continuous operation for over two hours by solving the “atom loss” problem using optical lattice conveyor belts. The Harvard system reloaded 50 million atoms over two hours while maintaining quantum information.

Together, these achievements demonstrate that multiple hardware modalities are making rapid progress. Neutral atoms, superconducting circuits, trapped ions, and silicon spin qubits are all advancing toward the threshold of error-corrected quantum computing. The question is no longer whether any single approach will win—it’s which problems each modality will solve best.

The $2.5 Billion Month¶

September 2025 will be remembered as the month quantum computing funding went mainstream. Three companies raised over $1.9 billion in private capital, achieving valuations that rival established technology companies. But the more significant signal may be NVIDIA’s strategic pivot: after years of skepticism, the GPU giant made three major quantum investments in a single month.

The Three Giants¶

PsiQuantum led the fundraising parade with a $1 billion Series E that values the company at $7 billion . The round, led by BlackRock, Temasek, and Baillie Gifford, included strategic investment from NVIDIA’s venture arm. The capital will fund construction of utility-scale quantum computing sites in Brisbane and Chicago, targeting a 2027 operational date for the Brisbane system. PsiQuantum’s photonic approach aims to build million-qubit systems using semiconductor manufacturing processes.

Quantinuum, the Honeywell-controlled trapped-ion leader, secured $600 million in equity funding at a $10 billion pre-money valuation. New investors included Quanta Computer, NVIDIA’s NVentures, and QED Investors. The funding will support the 2025 launch of the Helios quantum computer and progress toward the Apollo fault-tolerant system planned for 2029. Quantinuum also announced selection for DARPA’s Quantum Benchmarking Initiative, targeting utility-scale quantum computing by 2033.

IQM, the European superconducting quantum startup, raised $320 million in Series B funding achieving unicorn status with a $1 billion valuation. The company, founded in 2018, has sold 15 quantum computers and plans to use the funds for global expansion and software platform development. The round highlights growing European investor interest in quantum technology—currently, the EU accounts for only 5% of global private quantum funding compared to 50% for the United States.

Infleqtion announced a SPAC deal valuing the neutral-atom quantum computing firm at $1.8 billion , targeting $540 million in proceeds. The company also unveiled a new architecture accelerating its roadmap to 1,000 logical qubits by 2030.

NVIDIA’s Quantum Bet¶

The most significant strategic signal in September came from NVIDIA. After years of observing quantum computing from the sidelines, the company made three major quantum investments : PsiQuantum, Quantinuum, and an expanded partnership developing GPU-accelerated quantum simulation tools.

NVIDIA’s shift is telling. The company that powers the AI revolution is now betting that quantum computing will be a complementary technology for solving certain classes of problems. The company’s research collaborations achieved 50x speedups in error correction decoding and 4,000x performance boosts in quantum simulation, demonstrating that classical computing and quantum computing will advance in tandem.

The Government Wave¶

Public investment surged alongside private capital. New York State announced $300 million for a quantum hub at Stony Brook University. New Mexico committed $315 million to quantum computing initiatives. The National Science Foundation awarded $16 million for the design phase of the National Quantum Virtual Laboratory, including $4 million for a University of New Mexico-led team designing photonic quantum computers.

New Mexico partnered with DARPA to invest up to $240 million over four years in the Quantum Frontier Project, leveraging Sandia and Los Alamos national laboratories. The Maryland Quantum-Thermodynamics Hub secured over $5 million in combined state and private funding.

Hardware: Multiple Paths Forward¶

While NVIDIA’s quantum investments validate the industry’s commercial prospects, the hardware advances in September demonstrate that multiple technical paths are advancing in parallel. Quantum computing hardware progressed across all major modalities, with each approach solving different pieces of the scaling puzzle. The field is converging on a realization: different quantum computing technologies will likely serve different applications rather than a single winner-take-all outcome.

Modular & Superconducting¶

University of Illinois researchers developed modular quantum computers that “snap together like LEGO bricks” using superconducting qubits and coaxial cables. The system achieved approximately 99% SWAP gate fidelity, enabling scalable, reconfigurable architectures. Modular approaches address a fundamental challenge in quantum computing: as systems grow larger, the complexity of connecting all qubits becomes unmanageable. By building smaller modules and linking them, researchers can maintain high gate fidelities while scaling.

UC Berkeley and Northwestern scientists demonstrated a superconducting “grid states” qubit that intrinsically protects quantum information from decoherence. By integrating a Cooper-quartet junction with a phase-slip element in a high-impedance circuit, the team created robust qubit states that offer an alternative to active error correction.

Trapped Ion & Neutral Atom¶

Trapped-ion systems achieved what researchers call “unconditional quantum supremacy.” UT Austin and Quantinuum researchers proved that a 12-qubit quantum computer can complete a memory storage task that would require 62 to 382 bits of classical memory. This “quantum information supremacy” is permanent—no future classical algorithm or hardware improvement can close the gap because the result follows from information theory rather than computational complexity.

Neutral-atom systems continued their rapid advancement. Beyond Caltech’s record 6,100-qubit array, Harvard/MIT/QuEra researchers solved the atom loss problem that has limited continuous operation. Their 3,000-qubit system ran for over two hours by using optical lattice conveyor belts to rapidly resupply lost qubits without destroying quantum information. This capability is essential for error-corrected quantum computing, which requires long-duration coherent operations.

Silicon Spin Qubits¶

Silicon-based quantum computing achieved two breakthroughs in September. UNSW Sydney researchers demonstrated quantum entanglement between two atomic nuclei separated by 20 nanometers using a geometric gate method. The 76% fidelity entanglement allows nuclei to communicate via electrons even when not close together, enabling scaling compatible with standard silicon chip fabrication.

The system uses the hyperfine coupling of 111-113 MHz in phosphorus donors, with 12 MHz Heisenberg exchange interactions between electrons. The 2-microsecond controlled-Z gate operations and 99% nuclei readability demonstrate that silicon spin qubits can achieve the fidelities required for error correction while leveraging existing semiconductor manufacturing.

Researchers achieved 90% fidelity in generating 4-qubit cluster states , a foundational resource for measurement-based quantum computation using superconducting circuits. The study identified that dephasing noise severely degrades cluster states, providing guidance for targeted error-mitigation strategies.

Commercial: Going to Market¶

September marked a decisive shift from research prototypes to commercial products. Customers are now paying for quantum computers, deploying them in data centers, and using them to solve business problems.

Performance Milestones¶

IonQ announced its Tempo quantum computer achieved an algorithmic qubit (AQ) score of 64 , three months ahead of schedule. The AQ 64 system can access over 18 quintillion computational states, representing a 268 million-fold increase in computational space from AQ 36. The milestone demonstrates exponential scaling in quantum computing performance, with IonQ showing 35% improvement in QAOA solution quality, 74% improvement in Quantum Fourier Transform implementation, and 182% improvement in quantum search algorithms compared to competing systems.

Algorithmic qubits differ from physical qubits—they represent the effective computational power after accounting for error rates and gate fidelities. IonQ’s AQ 64 achievement suggests the company is on track toward its goal of 2 million qubits by 2030.

First Deployments¶

Commercial deployments accelerated geographically. Rigetti Computing announced approximately $5.7 million in purchase orders for two of its 9-qubit Novera quantum computing systems. One is destined for an Asian tech manufacturer developing internal expertise, the other for a California-based startup researching hardware and error correction. Both systems are upgradeable and expected for delivery in the first half of 2026.

Oxford Quantum Circuits installed New York City’s first quantum computer at a Digital Realty Trust data center in Manhattan. The British startup plans to spend tens of millions of dollars over three to five years integrating NVIDIA chips into the system, aiming to run AI programs more efficiently.

Czechia launched its first public quantum computer, VLK, at the IT4Innovations National Supercomputing Center in Ostrava. Built with 125 million Czech crowns in EU support, it is the second publicly available quantum computer in Europe. The system operates at close to -273 degrees Celsius and will be used for atmospheric monitoring research with the European Space Agency.

Manufacturing Breakthrough¶

The most significant commercial development came from Quantum Motion, which delivered the industry’s first full-stack silicon CMOS quantum computer to the UK National Quantum Computing Centre. The system uses standard 300mm silicon chip manufacturing—the same process used for conventional electronics—fitting into three 19-inch server racks.

The deployment demonstrates that quantum computers can be built using established, mass-producible semiconductor infrastructure. The system uses a modular “tile” architecture designed to scale to millions of qubits and is compatible with Qiskit and Cirq software frameworks. With two-qubit gate fidelities reaching 98% in prior research, silicon spin qubits are approaching the error-correction threshold while leveraging decades of manufacturing investment.

Quantum Motion’s system represents a cost-effective path to scaling because it uses existing chip fabs rather than requiring specialized manufacturing. The company’s architecture aims for millions of qubits per quantum processing unit, with a goal of 1 trillion quantum operations in error-corrected systems by 2035.

Applications: Solving Real Problems¶

Beyond hardware advances, September demonstrated that quantum computers are increasingly being applied to concrete challenges across finance, healthcare, and fundamental science.

Finance & Trading¶

HSBC, working with IBM, delivered the world’s first-known quantum-enabled algorithmic trading trial using real production-scale data from the European corporate bond market. The integration of IBM’s Heron quantum processor with classical systems achieved up to 34% improvement in predicting winning trades compared to classical methods alone.

The trial marks a practical step for quantum computing in financial applications, demonstrating that hybrid quantum-classical approaches can provide competitive advantages in algorithmic trading. HSBC used the system for bond price prediction, addressing one of the most liquid and complex markets in global finance.

Healthcare & Drug Discovery¶

SciSparc Ltd. announced an initiative to advance 3D protein modeling using quantum computing technology. The program aims to transform drug discovery by achieving unprecedented accuracy in predicting protein structures and interactions, targeting neurological and rare diseases.

University of Saskatchewan researchers are using quantum computers to simulate pathogens and test vaccine scenarios, aiming to produce vaccines in less than 100 days from the start of a new pandemic. The work partners the university’s quantum research center (quanTA) with the Vaccine and Infectious Disease Organization (VIDO), funded in part by federal grants and IBM Quantum Division access.

The research aims to radically shorten pandemic response times by using quantum simulation to model pathogen behavior and vaccine interactions. Their goal of sub-100-day vaccine development would represent a dramatic acceleration compared to traditional timelines.

Algorithmic Breakthroughs¶

Google Quantum AI researchers demonstrated “generative quantum advantage” for the first time , showing quantum computers can learn to generate outputs—like complex bitstrings or compressed circuits—that classical systems cannot replicate. The team used a 68-qubit superconducting processor and “instantaneously deep quantum neural networks” trained via a “sewing technique” to overcome optimization roadblocks.

The work moves quantum advantage beyond simple sampling toward trainable generative tasks, with potential applications in materials science and circuit optimization. The effective qubit count reached 816 in performance evaluations, with projections that 34,000 qubits would exceed classical reach.

Los Alamos National Laboratory and IBM researchers used a quantum algorithm to solve the century-old mathematical problem of factoring group representations, which was intractable for classical computers. The algorithm employs quantum Fourier transforms and has direct applications in particle physics, material science, and data transmission.

Partnerships: Building the Ecosystem¶

Big Tech Moves¶

Microsoft announced a new quantum research center at the University of Maryland’s Discovery District, featuring a prototype of Microsoft’s topological quantum computer. The center will serve as a hub for collaboration with government, academia, and industry, coinciding with Microsoft’s selection for the final phase of DARPA’s US2QC program.

NVIDIA expanded its quantum partnerships, with research achieving 2x speed improvements in quantum error correction decoding , 50x boosts in AI-powered decoders, and 4,000x performance improvements in quantum simulation methods. These advances in classical computing are critical for developing practical quantum applications.

Cisco Quantum Labs announced a unified quantum networking software stack and three research prototypes enabling distributed quantum computing across networked processors. The software includes a quantum compiler for partitioning circuits across multiple quantum computers and classical applications for eavesdropper detection and decision coordination. The company’s quantum network entanglement chip generates over 200 million entangled photon pairs per second.

Government & Academia¶

Government funding accelerated through multiple channels. The National Science Foundation selected four teams for the design phase of the National Quantum Virtual Laboratory, investing $16 million to provide researchers with shareable quantum computers and digital twins for testing algorithms.

New York State committed $300 million for a Quantum Research and Innovation Hub at Stony Brook University, leveraging Brookhaven National Laboratory and the university’s existing 170-mile quantum computing network. The hub aims to establish Long Island as a major quantum computing center.

New Mexico partnered with DARPA on the Quantum Frontier Project, investing up to $120 million each over four years to make large-scale quantum computers a reality by 2033. The initiative will leverage Sandia and Los Alamos national laboratories.

Global Expansion¶

QUDORA and Seoul-based Norma Inc. formed a strategic partnership to drive trapped-ion quantum computing adoption across South Korea’s ecosystem, targeting semiconductors and advanced manufacturing industries.

Rigetti Computing and India’s Centre for Development of Advanced Computing (C-DAC) signed an MOU to co-develop hybrid quantum computing systems , combining Rigetti’s superconducting technology with C-DAC’s high-performance computing expertise.

Qilimanjaro and Qblox expanded their collaboration to advance hybrid digital-analog quantum computing in Europe, integrating analog processors with digital superconducting systems at a new Barcelona data center opening in November.

Research: Theoretical Foundations¶

Error Correction & Noise¶

IBM Quantum researchers published a study challenging quantum’s “noise barrier” , showing that nonunital noise can be harnessed to extend computations beyond previous theoretical limits. Their “RESET” protocol uses this noise and ancillary qubits to correct errors without mid-circuit measurements, potentially enabling more powerful near-term devices.

The work challenges the prevailing view that noise is purely detrimental, suggesting current noisy processors may be more computationally capable than believed. By harnessing rather than fighting noise, IBM’s approach could accelerate the path to practical quantum advantage.

Researchers achieved 90% fidelity in generating 4-qubit cluster states , identifying that dephasing noise severely degrades cluster states while energy relaxation causes only modest fidelity loss. This insight enables targeted error-mitigation strategies for near-term devices.

Validation & Verification¶

UT Austin and Quantinuum researchers demonstrated unconditional quantum supremacy, proving that a 12-qubit system can complete a memory storage task that would require 62 to 382 bits of classical memory. This “quantum information supremacy” is the first that cannot be closed by future classical algorithm or hardware improvements because it derives from information theory rather than computational complexity.

Swinburne University researchers developed methods to validate outputs of Gaussian Boson Sampler quantum computers, which solve problems that would take 9,000 years on supercomputers. The team validated a GBS experiment and found errors requiring further investigation, aiming to ensure quantum computers retain their “quantumness” for commercial applications.

Theory & Materials¶

Perimeter Institute researchers provided a blueprint for understanding quantum computer speed limits by directly linking computational problem complexity to required quantum entanglement. The hardest problems require creating and manipulating highly complex webs of entanglement, which sets fundamental speed limits for quantum computation.

Japanese researchers observed “heavy fermions”—electrons with enhanced mass—exhibiting quantum entanglement governed by Planckian time in the material CeRhSn. The discovery suggests such entangled states in solid-state materials could be harnessed for novel quantum computing architectures.

Utah State University mathematicians developed non-semisimple topological quantum field theories predicting new quantum particles called “neglectons” that may offer more stable qubits, reducing errors from environmental noise.

Emerging Technologies & Infrastructure¶

Supporting technologies advanced alongside quantum processors, addressing critical bottlenecks in scaling.

Qubic, a Canadian startup, developed a cryogenic traveling-wave parametric amplifier that reduces quantum computer heat emissions by a factor of 10,000. The innovation virtually eliminates heat loss from a key component, potentially reducing running costs and removing a major barrier to scalable quantum systems. The company plans to bring the amplifier to market in 2026.

Quantum Source unveiled ORIGIN, a core engine for scalable photonic quantum computers using Cavity-QED technology to deterministically generate entangled cluster states. The system enables room-temperature quantum computers scaling to millions of qubits, with first product delivery expected by end of 2026.

Delft Circuits announced an I/O roadmap to scale quantum computers to thousands of qubits by addressing connectivity challenges. Their Cri/oFlex cables use superconducting materials to replace coaxial cabling, with projections to increase channel density from 256 to 4,096 channels by 2029.

Quantum Computing Inc. debuted a Quantum Secure Solution using high-dimensional time-energy entangled quantum modes , offering enhanced data density, scalability, and noise resilience. Operating at room temperature, the system integrates with existing telecom infrastructure.

**Maybell Quantum closed a $40 million Series B funding round](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/maybell-quantum-closes-40m-funding-round-for-quantum-computing-fridges/ ) to accelerate commercialization of its cryogenic systems for quantum computing, following a $25 million Series A in March 2024.

Market Signals & Industry Analysis¶

Financial markets sent strong signals about quantum computing’s commercial trajectory.

Defiance’s QTUM Quantum Computing ETF surpassed $2 billion in assets under management, highlighting growing investor interest in quantum technologies. The ETF, launched in 2018, holds companies like D-Wave Quantum, IonQ, and Rigetti Computing, and carries a 5-star Morningstar Rating.

Analyst firm B. Riley raised IonQ’s 12-month price target to $100, a new street-high reflecting confidence in the company’s path to scalable quantum advantage. IonQ’s stock has gained 805% over the past year, with the company reporting $20.7 million in Q2 2025 revenue (beating expectations by 15%) and forecasting $82-100 million for full year 2025.

A market report projects the global photonic quantum computing market will reach $1.1 billion by 2030 and over $7 billion by 2036, highlighting the rapid growth and commercialization of photonic approaches. The report identifies key advantages including room temperature operation and scalability.

Industry leaders from Fujitsu, Oxford Instruments, Quantinuum, and D-Wave emphasized during a major webinar that quantum computing is transitioning from lab research to commercial applications, with a focus on hybrid systems that complement rather than replace classical computers.

September in Context¶

September 2025 rewrote the quantum computing record books. The 6,100-qubit Caltech array shattered scaling barriers. Over $2.5 billion in capital flooded into startups, led by PsiQuantum’s $1 billion round and Quantinuum’s $10 billion valuation. NVIDIA made three strategic quantum investments, signaling that the AI and quantum computing paths are converging. HSBC demonstrated the world’s first quantum-enabled bond trading. And quantum computers are now being manufactured in standard silicon chip foundries.

The field is transitioning from scientific curiosity to engineering challenge. Multiple hardware modalities—neutral atoms, superconducting circuits, trapped ions, silicon spin qubits, photonics—are all advancing in parallel. This diversity is strength, not weakness. Different approaches will likely serve different applications: neutral atoms for simulation, trapped ions for algorithms requiring deep circuits, silicon spin qubits for mass deployment, photonics for networking and room-temperature operation.

The question isn’t “if” quantum computers will be useful anymore—it’s “when” and “for what.” NERSC and Berkeley Lab forecast that quantum computers could become practical tools for U.S. Department of Energy scientific workloads within the next decade. IBM’s roadmap targets 200 logical qubits by 2029 and 2,000 logical qubits by 2033. PsiQuantum aims for million-qubit systems by 2027.

But the path remains long. Fault-tolerant quantum computing requires orders of magnitude improvement in qubit quality, error correction, and system integration. September’s achievements demonstrate rapid progress, but the gap between today’s systems and fault-tolerant computers remains substantial.

What changed in September is the perception of quantum computing’s trajectory. The funding surge, commercial deployments, and real-world applications collectively signal that the industry has entered a new phase. The era of laboratory experiments is giving way to commercial deployment. The race is no longer about demonstrating quantum advantage—it’s about delivering quantum utility at scale.

September was extraordinary. But the work ahead is measured in years, not months.
