This comprehensive review covers 205 articles published in September 2025, surveying the latest developments in AI agents. Google launched an open payments protocol enabling autonomous agent-to-agent transactions. Startups raised over $1 billion in funding, led by Sierra’s $350M round and Cognition’s $400M raise. But as enterprises deploy agents at scale, security vulnerabilities emerged—researchers disclosed 25 critical vulnerabilities in the Model Context Protocol, and Salesforce revealed AI agents can be forced to leak sensitive data.

The Infrastructure Play¶

Google launched an open payments protocol enabling AI agents to transact directly with one another, using stablecoins for settlement. The protocol allows autonomous agents to commerce without human intervention—a foundational capability for the agentic economy. As American Banker noted , banks need to understand how this protocol could transform payment infrastructure when agents become economic actors.

The protocol addresses a core bottleneck in agentic systems: agents can’t autonomously buy and sell without payment capabilities. Google’s solution uses stablecoins to enable instant settlement, giving agents a way to hold and transfer value. Ledger Insights reported that this represents one of the first major infrastructure plays for agent-to-agent commerce at scale.

Visa also moved into the agentic commerce space, introducing new tools for developers building AI agents that engage in commerce. The company’s Instant Checkout and Agentic Commerce Protocol aim to standardize how agents participate in retail transactions.

OpenAI announced Instant Checkout and an Agentic Commerce Protocol, enabling ChatGPT to complete purchases directly within conversations. The integration removes friction from AI-assisted shopping—agents can now move from recommendation to transaction without switching contexts.

Mastercard released a guide to agentic retail , explaining how “agentic commerce” works and what retailers need to prepare for as AI agents become shopping assistants. The company framed agents as the next evolution of e-commerce, where AI handles discovery, comparison, and purchase on behalf of consumers.

This infrastructure layer matters because it enables the agent economy to function. Agents need to transact autonomously to be useful—from buying supplies to paying for API calls. September’s developments suggest the payment rails are being built in parallel with agent capabilities.

Billion-Dollar Bet¶

AI agent startups raised over $1.1 billion in September 2025, with two mega-rounds dominating the landscape and a long tail of specialized vertical applications attracting funding.

Mega-Rounds: Horizontal Platforms¶

Sierra raised $350 million at a $10 billion valuation, led by Greenoaks Capital. The company, co-founded by former Salesforce co-CEO Bret Taylor, builds customer service AI agents for enterprises. The $10B valuation places Sierra among the most valuable private AI agent companies, reflecting investor confidence in horizontal agent platforms that can serve multiple enterprise use cases.

Cognition raised $400 million to bolster its AI agent efforts. The company, known for its Devin software engineering agent, is building horizontal AI agents for coding and development tasks. The round brings total funding to nearly $1B for the company, which competes in the crowded AI software engineering space.

Vertical Specialists: Finance & Operations¶

Motion raised $60 million for AI agents designed for small businesses. TechCrunch reported Motion is building “the Microsoft Office of AI agents”—a suite of agents for common business workflows. The company is Y Combinator-backed and targets small business productivity with result-based pricing.

Tabs raised $55 million for AI agents serving finance teams. The company’s agents automate financial workflows, reconciliation, and reporting—targeting a pain point that requires accuracy and compliance. The round reflects investor interest in vertical AI agents that solve specific business problems.

Paid raised a $21 million seed round for a “results-based billing” model for AI agents. Founded by Manny Medina (former Outreach CEO), Paid only charges when its agents successfully complete tasks. The unusual pricing model addresses enterprise skepticism about AI agent ROI—customers pay for outcomes, not seat licenses. Lightspeed and FUSE led the round, with Lightspeed publishing an analysis of the "$19 trillion problem" in the AI agent economy that Paid aims to solve.

Seed Stage: Infrastructure & Security¶

Envive raised $15 million to build AI agents for online retailers. The Seattle startup’s agents handle customer service, product recommendations, and post-purchase support for e-commerce sites.

InCountry raised $10 million and launched AgentCloak, an AI agent data protection platform. The company’s technology helps enterprises comply with data residency regulations when agents cross borders—addressing a looming compliance challenge as agents access data globally.

Geordie emerged from stealth with $6.5 million for an AI agent security platform. The startup focuses on securing agent deployments, reflecting growing concern about agent vulnerabilities.

uiAgent raised $4.6 million to accelerate AI agent automation for accounting firms. The company’s agents handle tax preparation, bookkeeping, and client communication—addressing the accounting industry’s labor shortage.

Actual AI raised $3.2 million to build AI agents for engineering managers. The agents help with code reviews, sprint planning, and team coordination.

The funding pattern is clear: investors are betting on specific vertical applications (HR, accounting, finance, security) rather than general “agent platforms.” Sierra and Cognition are the outliers—horizontal agent platforms commanding premium valuations. Most capital is flowing to companies solving concrete business problems with agents, not building general-purpose agent infrastructure.

Amazon: Ads & Seller Experience¶

Amazon debuted an AI agent that can create ads from start to finish for its platforms. As ADWEEK reported , the ad agent handles creative generation, copywriting, and campaign management—reducing the friction for advertisers to launch campaigns on Amazon’s properties.

The company also launched a seller agent to help third-party sellers manage their businesses. According to Amazon’s announcement, the agent helps with inventory management, pricing decisions, and customer service—tasks that previously required significant seller time. CNBC noted the agent arrives as sellers face supply chain disruptions and trade policy uncertainty, making automation more attractive.

Microsoft: Office 365 & Teams¶

Microsoft added AI agents to Word, Excel, and PowerPoint , extending Copilot from chat interface to autonomous action. The agents can research, draft documents, analyze data, and create presentations—handling multi-step workflows that previously required human direction.

The company also filled Teams with AI agents , including agents for meeting preparation, recap, and follow-up tasks. Microsoft’s blog announced “Agent Mode and Office Agent” in Microsoft 365 Copilot, positioning agents as the evolution of productivity software.

Oracle expanded Fusion HCM with 13 new AI agents for HR automation. The agents handle performance reviews, career pathing, onboarding, and employee questions—automating many routine HR tasks while providing insights to managers. Oracle’s announcement positioned the agents as “AI-powered HR assistants” that boost workforce productivity and enhance performance management.

Workday expanded AI agents to HR, Finance, and specific industries . The company launched Workday Data Cloud and Workday Build, alongside more AI agents for contingent work, finance operations, and industry-specific workflows. Staffing Industry Analysts noted that Workday’s new agents include one specifically focused on contingent work—reflecting the growing gig economy workforce.

Indeed unveiled AI agents for job seekers and recruiters . The job platform’s agents help candidates find relevant openings, prepare applications, and practice interviews—while giving recruiters tools to screen candidates and schedule interviews automatically.

Productivity & Content¶

Notion launched AI agents that can research, write, and run workflows . The company’s workflow agents handle knowledge management, content creation, and task automation—extending Notion from a note-taking tool into an autonomous work platform. The agent can research topics across the web, draft documents, and execute multi-step workflows within Notion.

Box debuted AI agents for content management . The agents handle document classification, metadata extraction, contract analysis, and content routing—addressing the enterprise challenge of managing unstructured data at scale.

Adobe announced general availability of AI agents for customer experience orchestration. The company’s Agent Orchestrator and AI agents help businesses create personalized customer journeys across channels. As CIO reported , Adobe’s agents can analyze customer behavior, trigger personalized experiences, and coordinate across marketing, sales, and service touchpoints.

Genesys launched AI agents with greater autonomy for customer experience orchestration. The contact center platform’s agents handle customer inquiries, resolve issues, and escalate complex cases to humans—operating with more independence than previous chatbot-style systems.

ServiceNow launched the Zurich release, inserting process and task mining into AI agent workflows. The integration gives agents visibility into business processes, allowing them to identify bottlenecks and optimization opportunities.

The enterprise launch wave shows that incumbents are integrating agents into existing products rather than creating standalone agent platforms. This approach reduces adoption friction—users get agent capabilities within tools they already use. But it also means agents will be embedded in proprietary ecosystems, raising questions about interoperability and data portability.

Security Reality Check¶

As enterprises deploy AI agents at scale, security vulnerabilities have emerged that could undermine trust in the technology. September brought sobering news: researchers disclosed 25 critical vulnerabilities in the Model Context Protocol (MCP), Salesforce revealed AI agents can be manipulated to leak sensitive data, and reports surfaced of malicious agent servers stealing emails.

Critical Vulnerabilities Exposed¶

Security researchers found 25 vulnerabilities in MCP , the protocol that enables AI agents to connect to external tools and data sources. The vulnerabilities expose how agents can be exploited through the protocol itself—raising questions about whether the foundation of agent infrastructure is secure.

The MCP vulnerabilities are particularly concerning because the protocol is rapidly becoming a standard for agent-tool integration. If agents can’t safely connect to tools without being exploited, the entire agent ecosystem faces a bottleneck. The disclosures came as the World Economic Forum warned that unsecured AI agents expose businesses to new cyberthreats.

Salesforce disclosed that its AI agents can be forced to leak sensitive data . Researchers found that adversarial inputs can trick agents into bypassing security controls and exposing information they shouldn’t. The disclosure is notable because Salesforce has been aggressive in deploying agents—CEO Marc Benioff said agents now handle 50% of the company’s work.

Reports emerged of a malicious AI agent server stealing emails . The incident highlights how bad actors are already weaponizing agent infrastructure—creating rogue agents that exfiltrate data under the guise of legitimate AI services.

Additional research found that seemingly harmless photos could be used to hack AI agents . The attack technique involves embedding malicious instructions in images that agents process—bypassing text-based filters. TechRadar warned that IT can’t afford blind trust when agents go rogue.

CISOs Not Ready¶

Fast Company reported that AI agents are “breaking bad” and CISOs aren’t ready . The article documented how security teams lack visibility into agent behavior, can’t audit agent decisions, and don’t have tools to police agent-to-agent interactions. Help Net Security similarly noted that “AI agents are here, now comes the hard part for CISOs.”

Zscaler’s analysis examined the challenge of balancing speed and security in AI agent deployments—arguing that security can’t be an afterthought when agents have access to sensitive systems and data.

Computer Weekly asked whether AI agents are a blessing or curse for cybersecurity. The article noted that while agents can help defend systems, they also create new attack surfaces that defenders must monitor and protect.

Industry Response: New Security Platforms¶

The industry is responding with new security platforms and governance frameworks:

Astrix Security announced the first AI Agent Control Plane (ACP) for secure-by-design agent deployment. The ACP provides centralized visibility into agent permissions, data access, and connections—giving security teams a way to police agent behavior at scale. The Hacker News described how Astrix’s platform helps secure the “agentic era.”

Airia launched Agent Constraints , the industry’s first policy engine for centralized AI agent governance. The platform lets enterprises set guardrails on what agents can do, what data they can access, and how decisions are reviewed—addressing the control problem that plagues agent deployments.

Covasant launched an AI Agent Control Tower to “bring order to the chaotic enterprise AI landscape.” The platform provides visibility, governance, and management capabilities for enterprises running multiple agents from different vendors.

Geordie’s $6.5 million seed round for an AI agent security platform reflects growing investment in agent security as a category.

Lasso Platform employs AI agents to secure AI applications , using agent technology to police agent behavior—a “fight fire with fire” approach to security.

The Hacker News published guidance on how to gain control of AI agents and non-human identities, noting that traditional identity management approaches don’t work when agents create their own sub-identities and permissions.

CrowdStrike debuted threat-hunting AI agents and partnered with Salesforce on security solutions for AI agents and applications. The company is positioning itself as a security provider for the agentic era.

Wipro integrated Simbian’s AI agent into its managed security services, reflecting how security providers are incorporating agents into their operations.

AI Magazine reported on Boomi’s argument that AI agent governance “can’t wait”—arguing that enterprises need governance frameworks before deploying agents at scale.

The security developments in September reveal a tension: enterprises want to deploy agents quickly, but the security infrastructure is still catching up. The MCP vulnerabilities and data leak disclosures are setbacks, but the rapid response from security vendors suggests the industry is treating security seriously, not as an afterthought.

Governance and Control¶

As enterprises deploy hundreds of AI agents, they need infrastructure to manage, monitor, and control them at scale. September saw the emergence of governance platforms, observability tools, and enterprise infrastructure for agent management—the “pickaxes and shovels” layer of the agent boom.

Policy Engines & Control Planes¶

Airia’s Agent Constraints policy engine enables centralized AI agent governance by letting enterprises define what agents can and cannot do. The platform addresses a fundamental challenge: how do you set policy for autonomous systems that are designed to act independently?

Covasant’s AI Agent Control Tower provides visibility into agent behavior across the enterprise, giving administrators a single pane of glass to monitor and manage agents from different vendors.

OneTrust announced AI agents and new capabilities to deliver “AI-ready governance”—extending its privacy and compliance platform to handle AI agent deployments.

Observability & Monitoring¶

Monte Carlo launched Agent Observability to help teams build reliable AI systems. The platform provides monitoring, debugging, and quality assurance for AI agents—extending data observability to the agentic realm.

AWS Bedrock AgentCore Observability helps build trustworthy AI agents with monitoring tools that track agent decisions, data usage, and performance over time.

Temporal and OpenAI launched AI Agent Durability with a public preview integration that helps agents recover from failures and maintain state across long-running workflows.

Enterprise Infrastructure¶

Microsoft’s Agent Factory focuses on safe and secure agent development , providing a blueprint for enterprises building agents. The company also published guidance on connecting agents, apps, and data with open standards like MCP and A2A, and developer tools for rapid agent development .

Microsoft Marketplace promises simpler AI agent purchasing , giving enterprises a centralized way to discover and buy agent capabilities from multiple vendors.

The Cross App Access Protocol makes AI agents enterprise-ready by providing a standard way for agents to access applications across an enterprise without hard-coded integrations.

Salesforce’s MuleSoft Agent Fabric turns AI agents into enterprise infrastructure , providing integration and connectivity capabilities that let agents work with enterprise data and systems safely.

Deloitte published analysis arguing that scaling AI agents may be risky without an enterprise marketplace to help organizations discover, evaluate, and procure agent capabilities.

PwC published a guide on validating multi-agent AI systems , covering modular testing and system-level governance—a framework for ensuring agent reliability as systems grow complex.

The governance and control infrastructure emerging in September suggests enterprises are preparing for agent deployments at scale. Control planes, observability platforms, and standard protocols are the unsung infrastructure that makes large-scale agent deployments possible.

What September Means¶

September 2025 marked a phase transition for AI agents—from concept to deployment, from experimental to essential. The developments of the month reveal five patterns shaping the agentic future.

1. Infrastructure First¶

The industry is building rails before running fast. Google’s payments protocol, Visa’s commerce tools, and the emergence of governance platforms all suggest that infrastructure is being laid before explosive adoption. This is the opposite of typical tech hype cycles—usually products come first, infrastructure second. The agent ecosystem is being built methodically, with payment protocols, security platforms, and governance frameworks emerging alongside the first generation of products.

2. Verticalization Wins¶

Investors are funding specific use cases, not general agent platforms. Sierra and Cognition are exceptions that prove the rule—horizontal platforms at eye-popping valuations. But most capital is flowing to vertical applications: accounting (uiAgent, Paid), finance (Tabs), retail (Envive), healthcare (Hyro), security (Geordie). The pattern suggests enterprises want agents that understand their industry, not general-purpose assistants that require extensive customization.

3. Security Is the Bottleneck¶

The MCP vulnerabilities and Salesforce data leaks are setbacks, but they’re also forcing the industry to take security seriously. The rapid emergence of security platforms (Astrix ACP, Airia Agent Constraints, Covasant Control Tower) suggests vendors are treating security as a product requirement, not an afterthought. The question is whether security can catch up to deployment speed—CISOs clearly aren’t ready, as Fast Company reported, but the tools to address the gap are appearing rapidly.

4. Enterprise Integration Is Key¶

Microsoft, Oracle, Workday, Amazon, Notion, Box, Adobe— incumbents are embedding agents into existing products, not selling standalone agent platforms. This approach reduces adoption friction but also raises questions about lock-in and interoperability. The Cross App Access Protocol and other standards suggest the industry recognizes the need for interoperability, but proprietary agent ecosystems may still emerge.

5. The Agentic Organization Is Emerging¶

McKinsey published “The agentic organization: contours of the next paradigm” and “One year of agentic AI: six lessons” , arguing that agents aren’t just tools—they’re changing how work gets done. MIT Sloan Management Review published “Agentic AI at scale: redefining management for a superhuman workforce” , examining how organizations must adapt to manage autonomous AI workers.

CIO published articles on “rethinking the IT organization for the agentic AI era” and “taming AI agents: the autonomous workforce of 2026” —reflecting how IT leaders are preparing for agent deployments.

Bessemer Venture Partners published an “AI agent autonomy scale” —a new way to understand use case maturity and agent capabilities.

SaaStr published a practical “AI Agent Playbook” documenting how the company deployed 20+ agents to scale 8-figure revenue with single-digit headcount. Another SaaStr article examined whether AI agents can really manage other agents —concluding that while agent-on-agent management is possible, it creates “more work, not less” in the near term.

Readiness Gaps and Failure Rates¶

DesignRush reported that over 40% of AI agent projects will fail —and offered strategies to beat the odds. The failure rate is typical for emerging technologies, but it suggests the agent honeymoon is ending and the hard work of proving ROI is beginning.

Financial Management magazine found that executives embrace AI agents despite readiness gaps—highlighting the tension between enthusiasm for agent capabilities and preparation for deployment challenges.

CIO reported that “AI agents are coming. Your data isn’t ready”—arguing that data infrastructure is a bottleneck for agent deployments.

HR Executive published multiple articles on the organizational implications of agents: “Why every AI agent needs a human manager and clear job description” , “5 things to think about before bringing AI agents into the organization” , and “AI agents: where are they now? From proof of concept to success stories” .

The World Economic Forum argued that agentic AI presents a turning point for business resilience—while The Economist published a warning from Meredith Whittaker that “AI agents are coming for your privacy.”

IT Pro published a skeptical take , arguing that businesses are being oversold on agent capabilities.

Looking Forward: What Comes Next¶

September’s developments suggest Q4 2025 will focus on three priorities:

  1. Governance: Enterprises that deployed agents experimentally in Q3 will build governance frameworks in Q4. Control planes, observability platforms, and policy engines will move from nice-to-have to must-have.

  2. Security Hardening: The MCP vulnerabilities will prompt security reviews across agent deployments. Expect enterprises to demand security certifications, penetration testing, and compliance audits before trusting agents with sensitive data.

  3. ROI Proof: With 40% of projects at risk of failure, enterprises will pressure vendors to prove ROI. Results-based pricing models (like Paid’s) will become more common as customers refuse to pay for agents that don’t deliver measurable outcomes.

The honeymoon period for AI agents is ending. September showed that the technology is real—funding is flowing, products are shipping, enterprises are deploying. But the honeymoon is ending, and now comes the hard work of making agents reliable, secure, and valuable at scale.

BCG noted that AI agents are transforming consumer goods, while IBM argued that designing workflows where humans and agents thrive is the key to successful deployment.

Japan’s Tokio Marine and OpenAI announced a partnership on developing AI agents—reflecting international interest in the technology beyond the US market.

The Times predicted “downloadable employees” as the next workforce revolution, while ITPro asked whether workers should prepare to become AI agent bosses.

CNBC reported that companies are spending big on agentic AI “without always knowing what it does”—highlighting the knowledge gap that still exists between agent vendors and enterprise buyers.

AIMultiple published “12 reasons AI agents still aren’t ready in 2026” , while Visionary Marketing offered “AI agents, beyond the hype” —both attempting to ground expectations in reality.

September 2025 was a landmark month for AI agents—not because any single breakthrough occurred, but because the technology moved from concept to deployment at enterprise scale. The funding, product launches, security vulnerabilities, and governance developments all point to an ecosystem that is growing up quickly. The question for Q4 is whether the industry can address the security, governance, and ROI challenges before enterprises lose patience with the experimentation phase.

The infrastructure is being built, the products are shipping, and the money is flowing. Now comes the hard part: making AI agents reliable enough to trust with business-critical work. September showed both the promise and the peril of the agentic future—progress is real, but so are the challenges ahead.
