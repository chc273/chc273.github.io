This comprehensive review covers 185 articles published in April 2025, surveying the latest developments in quantum computing. This month brought remarkable progress in quantum reliability: Alice & Bob demonstrated cat qubits with 160x fewer errors , while researchers unveiled the first comprehensive benchmark showing quantum systems surpassing classical simulation limits. Beyond the lab, governments committed hundreds of millions, and new quantum systems came online worldwide.

Reliability Breakthrough: Cat Qubits Get 160x Better¶

The most significant technical advance this month came from Alice & Bob, which achieved a 160x reduction in bit-flip errors using “squeezed” cat qubits. The technique extends cat qubit lifetime from 138 milliseconds to 22 seconds—a dramatic improvement that makes error correction considerably less resource-intensive. Bit-flip errors, where a qubit spontaneously flips from \(|0\rangle\) to \(|1\rangle\), are one of the two fundamental error types in quantum computing. Suppressing them at the hardware level reduces the overhead required for software-based error correction.

The squeezing technique works without modifying circuit design, making it potentially applicable to existing cat qubit architectures. Cat qubits are a type of bosonic code that encodes quantum information in the oscillations of a superconducting resonator, named after Schrodinger’s famous thought experiment. By squeezing the quantum state, the researchers effectively narrowed the uncertainty in one quadrature while expanding it in the orthogonal direction—trading one type of error for another that’s easier to correct.

Complementary Error Correction Advances¶

Alice & Bob’s breakthrough is part of a broader set of error correction advances this month:

  • University of Twente researchers invented a photonic error correction method that filters high-quality photons at the source rather than correcting errors after the fact. Previous approaches required hundreds of physical photons to create a single reliable logical qubit; the new technique dramatically reduces this overhead, making photonic quantum computers cheaper and more practical.

  • Scientists at the University of Witwatersrand developed optical skyrmions —topological structures formed in entangled photon pairs that encode quantum information in their global properties rather than local states. Even when entanglement degrades, the topological protection helps preserve information, potentially enabling more robust quantum networks.

  • Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory researchers reduced superinductor capacitance by over 60% through a new chemical etching process that suspends superinductors above the wafer surface. This cuts substrate-related noise, a major source of error in superconducting qubits, and provides a practical fabrication method for scaling up.

  • UCL engineers broke a 25-year barrier in chip fabrication by using arsenic atoms instead of phosphorus for single-atom placement. They achieved 97% accuracy in positioning individual atoms—compared to the 70% success rate typical for phosphorus—demonstrating a path toward near-zero failure rates in quantum chip manufacturing.

These advances represent complementary approaches to the same fundamental challenge: making quantum computations reliable enough to be useful. While each targets different qubit modalities—superconducting, photonic, topological, silicon-based—together they indicate rapid progress across the field.

Measuring Progress: When Do Quantum Computers Win?¶

A critical development this month came from the Jülich Research Centre, which unveiled a comprehensive benchmark comparing 19 quantum processing units from five suppliers (IBM, Quantinuum, IonQ, Rigetti, and IQM). Built around the MaxCut optimization problem, the benchmark provides a platform-agnostic way to measure quantum performance as systems scale.

The results revealed important differences between platforms. Quantinuum’s H2-1 passed the benchmark at 56 qubits—a scale that surpasses exact classical simulation. The test used 4,620 two-qubit gates in a Linear Ramp Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (LR-QAOA) circuit, demonstrating that trapped-ion systems can maintain coherence at useful scales.

IBM’s processors showed different strengths: the Fez chip handled 100-qubit problems at depths up to 10,000 layers, though coherent information was retained only through approximately 300 layers. More importantly, the newer Marrakesh chip demonstrated half the error rate per layered gate compared to Fez, showing rapid iterative improvement in IBM’s superconducting hardware.

Benchmarking Software Performance¶

Separately, researchers introduced Benchpress, an open-source benchmarking suite for quantum software development kits. The suite includes 1,066 tests covering circuit construction, manipulation, and compilation across SDKs including Qiskit, Cirq, Braket, Tket, and others. In initial tests, Qiskit completed circuit construction tests in 2.0 seconds while Tket required 14.2 seconds—but Tket produced circuits with fewer two-qubit gates for certain decompositions, highlighting different performance tradeoffs.

The Challenge of Meaningful Benchmarks¶

The quest for meaningful benchmarks also revealed ongoing controversies. IEEE Spectrum published a critique of D-Wave’s quantum supremacy claims , noting that the company’s annealing approach solves different problems than gate-based quantum computers and that direct comparisons are often misleading. New Scientist similarly examined why quantum computers continue to fail certain key tests, emphasizing that cross-entropy benchmarking—while useful for measuring random circuit sampling—may not capture performance on practical applications.

This tension between demonstrating raw quantum advantage and solving useful problems remains central to the field. The MaxCut benchmark represents progress because it’s based on a real optimization problem with commercial applications, rather than an artificial benchmark designed solely to showcase quantum speedups.

Building the Quantum Internet: QNodeOS and Distributed Computing¶

Quantum Operating System¶

Researchers unveiled QNodeOS, billed as the world’s first operating system for quantum computers . The system enables multiple quantum computers to be controlled centrally and connected for distributed quantum computing—a foundational capability for a future quantum internet. QNodeOS handles resource allocation, job scheduling, and network management across diverse quantum hardware, abstracting away the differences between systems from different vendors.

Network Infrastructure¶

The UK demonstrated a 410-kilometer quantum network integrating both quantum key distribution (QKD) and entanglement distribution . The network connects multiple nodes and represents a step toward practical quantum communication infrastructure. Simultaneously, the UK launched its Integrated Quantum Networks Hub at Heriot-Watt University, bringing together academia and industry to pioneer secure quantum networks .

In the United States, IonQ and the Electric Power Board (EPB) launched a $22 million Quantum Innovation Center in Chattanooga, Tennessee. The center will house IonQ quantum computing systems and establish the first U.S. hub dedicated to quantum computing and networking research.

New Deployments¶

IQM announced it will install Poland’s first superconducting quantum computer at Wroclaw University of Science and Technology. The system will support Polish research in quantum algorithms and materials science.

Hardware Milestones: New Systems Come Online¶

Japan¶

Fujitsu and RIKEN unveiled a 256-qubit superconducting quantum computer , quadrupling the qubit count of their previous 64-qubit system. The new system builds on Fujitsu’s ongoing collaboration with RIKEN and represents a significant step toward Japan’s goal of developing a domestically produced quantum computing stack.

Separately, ULVAC delivered a cryogenic system for Japan’s first fully domestic quantum computer , supporting the country’s push for end-to-end sovereign quantum capabilities.

India¶

QpiAI launched the QpiAI-Indus, India’s first full-stack 25-qubit quantum computer under the country’s National Quantum Mission. The system features superconducting qubits and includes both cloud access and on-premises deployment options. India’s quantum push has gained momentum following a government commitment of approximately $1 billion over five years.

Europe¶

Beyond the IQM deployment in Poland, several European quantum initiatives advanced this month. The UK’s quantum hubs continued their work on networking and applications, while Norway announced new investment in quantum technology research .

Mobile Systems¶

SaxonQ demonstrated applications running on a mobile quantum computer powered by Quantum Machines’ control electronics. The demonstration showed that room-temperature quantum computing—using nitrogen-vacancy centers in diamond—can operate outside specialized data center environments, opening possibilities for field-deployed quantum sensors and computers.

Research Highlights: From Quasiparticles to Topology¶

Mathematical Applications¶

Researchers used Quantinuum’s H2-2 quantum computer to explore knot theory , demonstrating how quantum systems can solve complex mathematical problems. The work involved implementing quantum algorithms to compute knot invariants—mathematical quantities that distinguish different types of knots—with potential applications in topological quantum computing and quantum field theory.

In a separate study, researchers demonstrated quantum computing for contingency analysis in power systems , showing how quantum algorithms could identify potentially dangerous configurations in electrical grids more efficiently than classical methods.

Physics Discoveries¶

National Science Foundation-funded researchers discovered a new quantum quasiparticle : fractional excitons, which carry fractions of an electron’s charge and could enable new approaches to quantum information storage and manipulation.

Researchers observed a superradiant phase transition that had been predicted for 50 years but never seen until now. The phenomenon, observed in a trapped-ion quantum simulator, could advance our understanding of open quantum systems and quantum thermodynamics.

Quantinuum’s H2 system was used to simulate quantum magnetism using digital quantum simulation techniques, providing insights into magnetic materials that are difficult to study classically.

Hardware Advances¶

Trapped ion systems achieved a milestone in mid-circuit measurement and reset , capabilities essential for error correction and algorithm efficiency. The ability to measure qubits mid-computation without disturbing neighboring qubits, then reset and reuse them, dramatically reduces the qubit overhead required for fault-tolerant quantum computing.

Practical Applications¶

Google Quantum AI published research on practical applications of its quantum computers , emphasizing work toward quantum advantage in materials simulation and optimization problems.

Researchers demonstrated quantum machine learning for predicting proton affinity , achieving superior accuracy compared to classical methods. Proton affinity is a key property in chemistry and drug discovery, with applications ranging from catalyst design to understanding biochemical reactions.

In China, researchers demonstrated quantum-assisted fine-tuning of billion-parameter AI models , illustrating the convergence of quantum computing and artificial intelligence—a trend that accelerated throughout 2025.

Government Bets: DARPA, UK, and Global Investment¶

DARPA Quantum Benchmarking Initiative¶

The U.S. Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) made significant moves in quantum computing this month. The agency’s Quantum Benchmarking Initiative (QBI) selected 18 companies for Stage A awards of approximately $1 million each. The winners include:

  • Atom Computing — Selected for neutral atom quantum computing exploration
  • IonQ — Selected to advance utility-scale quantum computing

The program targets utility-scale quantum systems by 2033 , with DARPA taking an agnostic approach to qubit modalities. Stage A requires companies to develop conceptual descriptions of how they would achieve a logical qubit error rate of \(10^{-6}\) or better—a threshold considered necessary for practical quantum advantage.

UK Quantum Investment¶

The UK government announced £121 million in funding for quantum applications in fraud detection, anti-money laundering, and cybersecurity . The investment targets practical applications where quantum computing could provide near-term value, though some analysts questioned whether the amount is sufficient given the scale of the challenge.

Other Government Initiatives¶

  • Norway announced new investment in quantum technology research , strengthening its national quantum capabilities.
  • Colorado received a $40.5 million federal grant for quantum technology development, solidifying the state’s position as a U.S. quantum hub.
  • Italy finalized its national quantum strategy , positioning the country to compete in the European quantum ecosystem.
  • The EU explored quantum computing applications in the energy sector, examining how quantum algorithms could optimize power grids .
  • National security concerns gained prominence, with analyses of quantum threats to nuclear command and control systems and the UK’s quantum security posture .

Industry and Partnerships: Quantum Goes Commercial¶

Healthcare & Research Partnerships¶

CAS (a division of the American Chemical Society) and Cleveland Clinic formed a strategic collaboration to advance brain health research using AI and quantum computing. The partnership aims to shorten the typical 17-year timeline from lab discovery to approved therapy, with initial projects focusing on Alzheimer’s drug discovery.

The Milner Therapeutics Institute at Cambridge University signed an MOU with Yonsei University to foster joint quantum computing research. Yonsei will utilize Korea’s first IBM Quantum Computer for collaborative projects, aiming to synergize quantum computing with AI-driven drug discovery.

Investments & Funding¶

  • BTQ Technologies signed a term sheet to invest €2 million in QPerfect , a neutral atom quantum computing company. The investment grants BTQ a 16.67% equity stake with an option for full ownership within 12 months. QPerfect’s MIMIQ platform provides quantum design automation and simulation capabilities.

  • Rigetti Computing closed a $35 million strategic investment from Quanta Computer, finalizing the investment portion of their collaboration agreement. The partnership aims to combine Quanta’s manufacturing expertise with Rigetti’s superconducting quantum technology.

  • IQM, the Finnish quantum startup, is seeking approximately €128 million in a new funding round as the quantum computing industry sees increased investor interest.

  • IBM announced plans to spend approximately $150 billion on quantum computing over the coming decade, though analysts questioned whether the full amount represents direct quantum investment or broader computational infrastructure.

Technology Partnerships¶

  • Quantum Machines partnered with NVIDIA’s Accelerated Quantum Research Center to integrate quantum processors with AI supercomputers . The collaboration will use NVIDIA’s GB200 Grace Blackwell Superchips and QM’s OPX1000 control system to enhance quantum-classical communication.

  • Fujitsu expanded its quantum collaboration with RIKEN following the unveiling of their 256-qubit system, strengthening Japan’s domestic quantum capabilities.

  • Terra Quantum announced a strategic partnership with Siemens , targeting quantum applications in industrial optimization and sensing.

  • IonQ expanded its quantum collaboration in Japan through a memorandum of understanding with AIST (National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology), strengthening the company’s presence in the Asian market.

Government Contracts¶

  • Quantum Computing Inc. (QCi) was awarded a contract to support NASA’s space-based lidar data analysis , applying quantum algorithms to Earth observation data.

  • A UK consortium received a £4.7 million award targeting fault-tolerant quantum computing , accelerating research toward error-corrected quantum systems.

Industry Trends: Market Growth, AI Convergence, and Realistic Expectations¶

Market Projections¶

The quantum computing market is projected to reach $2.2 billion by 2027 , according to industry surveys. This growth is driven by increasing enterprise adoption, government investment, and maturing hardware platforms.

Survey data indicates that 83% of quantum professionals expect quantum utility to arrive within a decade , reflecting growing confidence in the field’s trajectory. However, expectations vary significantly by sector and application area.

Quantum-AI Convergence¶

The convergence of quantum computing and artificial intelligence accelerated this month. Observers noted that quantum SDKs are evolving into Quantum AI SDKs , reflecting the growing integration of quantum computing with machine learning workflows. Google in particular is exploring how quantum computing could supercharge its AI ambitions , potentially using quantum systems for training optimization or inference acceleration.

Geographic Competition¶

The race for quantum advantage has distinct geographic dimensions. Chicago is positioning itself as a quantum hub with $500 million in state investment , leveraging its concentration of quantum research institutions and companies. Meanwhile, the U.S.-China competition in quantum technology continues to shape national strategies and investment priorities.

Sector-Specific Applications¶

Industry analyses explored quantum computing’s potential across sectors:

  • Finance: Quantum computing could deliver $450-850 billion in value by 2035 , according to market projections, with applications in portfolio optimization, risk analysis, and fraud detection.

  • Supply Chains: Quantum algorithms may improve supply chain optimization by solving complex logistics problems more efficiently than classical methods.

  • Cryptography: The impact of quantum computing on cryptography continues to drive urgency around post-quantum cryptography migration.

Realistic Expectations¶

Nature published a cautionary perspective on quantum hype , reminding readers that progress is incremental and that many announced “breakthroughs” represent engineering milestones rather than revolutionary leaps. The article emphasized the gap between qubit counts and useful quantum advantage, noting that error correction remains the fundamental challenge.

Similarly, CNBC questioned whether Google and IBM’s bullish quantum optimism is justified , given that practical quantum advantage remains unproven for most commercial applications. The article noted that while technical progress is real, timelines for commercial viability may be longer than company PR suggests.

World Quantum Day 2025 (April 14) provided an occasion for industry reflection, with various stakeholders weighing in on the state of quantum computing . The consensus view acknowledged significant technical progress while emphasizing that the field remains in early stages.

Looking Ahead: What April 2025 Tells Us¶

The developments of April 2025 reveal a field making measurable, incremental progress on multiple fronts. Error correction advances—Alice & Bob’s cat qubits, photonic distillation, superinductor improvements, atomically precise fabrication—are systematically reducing the error rates that have long limited quantum computations. New benchmarks like MaxCut and Benchpress are giving us better ways to measure that progress and compare different approaches objectively.

Hardware deployments are expanding both in scale and geography. Japan’s 256-qubit system, India’s first full-stack quantum computer, and Poland’s inaugural superconducting system demonstrate that quantum computing is becoming a global endeavor. Governments are placing serious bets: DARPA’s 2033 target for utility-scale systems, the UK’s application-focused £121 million investment, and various national strategies all signal institutional commitment to quantum advantage.

The convergence with AI is accelerating, though it remains unclear whether quantum systems will provide meaningful advantages for machine learning in the near term. The finance sector continues to lead in commercial experimentation, while healthcare partnerships like CAS-Cleveland Clinic suggest quantum computing may find early applications in drug discovery and molecular simulation.

Yet challenges remain. Nature’s critique of quantum hype serves as a useful reminder that technical milestones don’t automatically translate to commercial advantage. The gap between laboratory demonstrations and reliable, scalable quantum systems is still substantial. Error correction, while improving, remains resource-intensive. And meaningful benchmarks that measure practical advantage—rather than contrived problems—are only beginning to emerge.

The pattern is clear: progress is happening, but it’s engineering-focused and cumulative rather than sudden and revolutionary. DARPA’s 2033 target for utility-scale quantum computing seems increasingly plausible given the rate of improvement in error rates and system scale. But the question remains which approach—superconducting, trapped ion, neutral atom, photonic—will reach practical utility first.

April 2025 didn’t deliver a single “breakthrough” that changed everything. Instead, it delivered something more valuable: evidence that multiple quantum computing approaches are making steady progress on the fundamental challenges that have limited the field for years. That may be less exciting than headlines promise, but it’s the reality of quantum computing in 2025—and it’s enough to keep the field moving forward.
