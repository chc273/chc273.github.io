This comprehensive review covers 87 articles published between October 1-31, 2025, surveying the latest developments in quantum computing. This month, Caltech built a 6,100-qubit array at room temperature, Google unveiled verifiable quantum advantage with its Willow chip, and NVIDIA announced NVQLink to tightly couple GPUs with quantum processors. The field is making measurable progress on multiple fronts.

The Numbers: Scale and Speed¶

The most striking quantitative milestone of the month came from Caltech, where researchers led by professor Manuel Endres built a neutral-atom quantum array with 6,100 qubits - nearly 10 times larger than the previous record of 1,180 qubits. The system operates at room temperature and achieved a 12.6-second coherence time while maintaining 99.98% accuracy in manipulating individual atoms. The array uses approximately 12,000 laser tweezers arranged in a two-zone “zone-based quantum computing” architecture, with 6,139 atoms stably loaded into the grid.

The significance lies in the combination of scale and stability. Previous neutral-atom systems faced trade-offs between qubit count and coherence time. The Caltech team demonstrated that both can scale together: they maintained record coherence times for hyperfine qubits in an optical tweezer array while achieving qubit counts an order of magnitude beyond prior systems. The researchers estimate scaling to over 10,000 qubits in the near future, with a potential path to one million qubits within a decade.

Google’s Willow quantum chip, featuring a 105-qubit superconducting array, achieved what the company calls the first verifiable quantum advantage. Running the “Quantum Echoes” algorithm (measuring out-of-time-order correlators), Willow completed a calculation in 2 hours that would take an estimated 150 years on the Frontier supercomputer - a 13,000x speedup. The chip achieved 99.97% single-qubit gate fidelity, 99.88% entangling gate fidelity, and 99.5% readout fidelity, with gate operations running in tens to hundreds of nanoseconds.

However, researchers expressed skepticism about the broader implications. The calculation, while verifiable, remains highly specialized. The verification method itself requires another quantum computer, which limits immediate practical utility. Scott Aaronson at the University of Texas at Austin noted that this represents progress on a specific class of problems rather than general quantum advantage. The Nature news article covering the announcement highlighted ongoing debates about what constitutes meaningful quantum advantage and whether such demonstrations accelerate the path to practical applications.

The Real Bottleneck: Error Correction¶

Quantum computers are inherently error-prone; fixing these errors is the main obstacle to practical use. Multiple advances this month addressed this bottleneck from different angles.

Algorithmic Advances¶

Researchers published a new technique called Algorithmic Fault Tolerance (AFT) that can reduce error correction overhead by 10-100 times. The method, tested in simulations by QuEra , restructures algorithms to detect errors on the fly rather than pausing for separate correction cycles. Yuval Boger, QuEra’s chief commercial officer, said this “moves the timeline forward significantly” for practical quantum computers. The research results were published in Nature on September 24, with hardware tests expected in 2026-2027.

Hardware Improvements¶

IonQ announced a world-record two-qubit gate fidelity of 99.99%, achieved without resource-intensive ground-state cooling. This breakthrough addresses a major speed bottleneck in trapped-ion quantum computing. In previous QCCD (Quantum Charge Coupled Device) demonstrations, runtime was dominated by ion movement (~27%) and cooling (~68%), with only ~2% spent on actual quantum gate operations. The new approach allows faster ion cooling and movement, potentially enabling order-of-magnitude faster computations. IonQ’s roadmap targets scaling to millions of qubits by 2030.

Researchers at NTNU, Leiden University, and MIT developed a real-time calibration method that monitors qubits and continuously adjusts their frequency to counter environmental disturbances. The technique, dubbed “Binary-Search Hamiltonian Tracking,” uses an FPGA controller to extend qubit lifetime and enhance precision. Jacob Benestad, the lead researcher, compared it to “tuning a guitar string while it’s being played.”

IBM announced that conventional AMD processors can execute a key quantum error correction algorithm, suggesting that existing classical hardware may accelerate progress toward fault-tolerant systems by offloading error correction workloads.

At the Q2B25 conference in Paris, experts highlighted progress in quantum error correction codes and decoders. Panelists discussed concatenated codes, directional codes, and IBM’s bivariate bicycle codes. The discussion emphasized the target of achieving 1 million error-free quantum operations (MegaQuOp) and eventually 1 trillion (TeraQuOp), compared to current systems that manage only a few hundred operations before errors occur. The panel also noted that trapped-ion and neutral-atom platforms are approximately 1,000 times slower than superconducting and photonic qubits, creating trade-offs between speed and error rates.

Bridging Worlds: NVQLink and Hybrid Systems¶

NVIDIA’s announcement of NVQLink on October 28 represents a major infrastructure development. The open system architecture enables tight coupling between GPU supercomputers and quantum processors (QPUs). Seventeen quantum hardware builders , five controller builders, and nine U.S. national laboratories are involved in the initiative.

The QPU builders include Alice & Bob, Anyon Computing, Atom Computing, Diraq, Infleqtion, IonQ, IQM Quantum Computers, ORCA Computing, Oxford Quantum Circuits, Pasqal, Quandela, Quantinuum, Quantum Circuits Inc., Quantum Machines, Quantum Motion, QuEra, and Rigetti. Controller builders include Keysight Technologies, Qblox, QubiC, and Zurich Instruments. The participating national labs are Brookhaven, Fermilab, Lawrence Berkeley, Los Alamos, MIT Lincoln Laboratory, Oak Ridge, Pacific Northwest, Sandia, and Argonne.

NVQLink addresses a critical requirement for fault-tolerant quantum computing: error correction demands hybrid quantum-classical systems with extremely low latency. Alice & Bob , which uses cat qubits that it claims reduce hardware requirements by 200 times compared to competing approaches, will integrate NVQLink for GPU compilation, live decoding, and dynamic calibration. Infleqtion announced integration with its neutral-atom Sqale quantum computer, providing a standardized, low-latency connection for real-time hybrid computing.

Berkeley Lab partnered with NVIDIA to use its open-source QubiC platform to combine QPUs, GPUs, and CPUs for quantum error correction and hybrid algorithms. NVIDIA also revealed plans to deploy two AI-ready supercomputers, Solstice (with 100,000 Blackwell GPUs) and Equinox (with 10,000 Blackwell GPUs), at Argonne National Laboratory in 2026.

The hybrid approach is taking shape beyond NVIDIA as well. Saudi Aramco launched a quantum emulator designed to simulate quantum systems using classical computing resources, enabling researchers to test quantum algorithms before quantum hardware is available. The emulator aims to accelerate quantum research in energy applications.

Industry Movements¶

Consolidation¶

Google acquired Atlantic Quantum on October 2, absorbing the MIT-founded startup’s modular chip stack technology and team. Atlantic Quantum had developed an approach integrating qubits and control electronics in the same cold stage, aiming to streamline hardware and enhance scalability. The acquisition brings co-founders Bharath Kannan (CEO), Simon Gustavsson (CTO), Will Oliver, Jonas Bylander, Tim Menke, Youngkyu Sung, Leon Ding, and Shereen Shermak into Google Quantum AI. This reflects a broader trend of research-intensive startups being absorbed into larger corporate quantum programs.

Commercial Deployments¶

D-Wave signed a 10 million agreement with Swiss Quantum Technology SA to deploy an Advantage2 annealing quantum computer in Europe. The system features over 4,400 qubits and will be accessible via D-Wave’s Leap cloud service. SQT will have 50% access to the system for five years, supporting the newly launched Q-Alliance in Italy. The deployment aims to advance quantum computing research while addressing energy efficiency concerns.

IBM inaugurated the IBM Quantum System Two with a 156-qubit Heron processor in San Sebastian, Spain - the first such system in Europe and third globally. The Basque regional government invested 50 million ($58 million) in the facility. The Heron processor delivers 50 times increased capability, 16 times increased precision, and 25 times increased speed compared to previous systems. IBM’s quantum network now includes 700,000 developers. The center aims to reach “quantum utility” by 2026.

China launched a commercial superconducting quantum computer based on the Zuchongzhi 3.0 design, offering remote access via the Tianyan quantum cloud platform. Built by China Telecom Quantum Group and QuantumCTek, the system features a 105-qubit chip with 182 couplers. The Tianyan platform hosts an 880-qubit superconducting cluster across multiple 24-qubit and 176-qubit machines. The platform has received 37 million visits from users in 60 countries and has handled 2 million experiments since its November 2023 launch.

Quandela delivered “Lucy,” a 12-qubit universal digital photonic quantum computer, to CEA’s TGCC for the EuroHPC and GENCI consortium. The system will be coupled with the Joliot-Curie supercomputer and made accessible to European researchers starting in 2026. Notably, 80% of components are sourced within Europe, marking a step toward technological sovereignty. CEO Niccolo Somaschi noted the system took 12 months to assemble at Quandela’s facilities.

Technology Roadmaps¶

PsiQuantum announced plans to build a quantum supercomputer using light-based qubits, with a goal of scaling to one million qubits. The company has taken delivery of a cryogenic cabinet for the system at its new facility in Milpitas, California. The photonic approach aims to leverage existing semiconductor manufacturing for scalability.

Research Notes¶

Fundamentals¶

Three physicists won the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics for demonstrating quantum physics at the macroscopic scale through groundbreaking quantum-tunnelling experiments. The recognition highlights progress in showing quantum effects in larger systems than previously observed.

Researchers built a “quantum lie detector” using a 73-qubit superconducting quantum processor to achieve an energy state impossible under classical physics. The result was verified to 48 standard deviations below the classical minimum, certifying nonlocal correlations in groups of up to 24 qubits. The Bell test, performed by researchers at Leiden University, Tsinghua University, and Zhejiang University, proves the system operates according to quantum principles rather than classical approximations.

A Federal Reserve study warned that quantum computers pose a “harvest now, decrypt later” threat to Bitcoin and other blockchains. Encrypted data collected today could be decrypted in the future, exposing transaction histories and private keys. The study notes that even post-quantum cryptography cannot protect already harvested data. Federal agencies have been directed to begin migration to post-quantum cryptography by 2035.

Materials and Hardware¶

Penn State researchers developed strained barium titanate thin films (40 nanometers thick) that improve electron-to-photon conversion by over 10 times at cryogenic temperatures. The material, first discovered in 1941, could advance quantum networks and make data centers more energy efficient by enabling better microwave-to-optical conversion for quantum communications.

University of Chicago scientists developed molecular qubits made with erbium that are compatible with standard telecommunications wavelengths and silicon photonics. Published in Science, the research creates a nanoscale bridge between magnetic spin states and light, enabling potential quantum networks that integrate with existing optical fiber infrastructure. The work involved collaboration with UC Berkeley, Argonne National Laboratory, and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory.

Harvard researchers demonstrated continuous operation of a quantum processor, an important step toward practical quantum computers that can run sustained calculations rather than single-shot experiments. The advance addresses the need for quantum systems to maintain coherence over extended computation periods.

Algorithms and Applications¶

University at Buffalo researchers expanded the truncated Wigner approximation (TWA), enabling quantum simulations that once required supercomputers to run on regular laptops. The user-friendly framework allows physicists to set up and run simulations in days rather than dealing with pages of complex mathematics. The study was published in PRX Quantum in September 2025.

Researchers applied quantum computing approaches to quantum chemistry simulations and optimization problems, demonstrating progress toward practical applications. The work includes methods for simulating molecular systems and solving combinatorial optimization problems using quantum algorithms.

A study explored the fundamental limits of quantum computation, examining what types of problems quantum computers can and cannot solve efficiently. The research provides theoretical bounds on quantum advantage and helps distinguish between problems that benefit from quantum speedup and those that do not.

What This Month Means¶

The October developments show quantum computing progressing on multiple parallel tracks. The 6,100-qubit milestone demonstrates that neutral-atom platforms can scale without sacrificing coherence, challenging the assumption that qubit count and stability are fundamentally opposed. Google’s verifiable quantum advantage claim, while met with appropriate skepticism, represents progress toward demonstrating computations that classical systems cannot efficiently verify or perform.

The error correction advances - AFT’s 10-100x overhead reduction, IonQ’s 99.99% gate fidelity, and real-time calibration methods - directly address the primary obstacle to fault-tolerant quantum computing. These are incremental but measurable steps toward systems that can perform long, reliable computations.

NVQLink signals a broader industry shift toward hybrid quantum-classical architectures. With 17 quantum hardware builders and 9 national labs committing to the standard, the field is converging on a model where GPUs handle error correction, control, and data processing while QPUs execute quantum operations. This division of labor may accelerate progress by letting each platform do what it does best.

The industry movements - acquisitions, deployments, and new commercial systems - reflect growing confidence that quantum computing will eventually become practical. The competitive landscape is intensifying globally, with the U.S., Europe, and China each making strategic investments in quantum infrastructure.

The field is transitioning from “can we build it?” to “can we make it useful?” The timeline remains long - fault-tolerant quantum computers capable of solving commercially valuable problems are still years away. But progress is measurable across hardware, error correction, and system integration.
